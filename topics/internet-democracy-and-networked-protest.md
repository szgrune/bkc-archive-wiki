# Internet & Democracy — Networked Protest to Networked Propaganda

BKC's **Internet & Democracy Project**, run for over a decade by the same core team
— Bruce Etling, Robert Faris, John Kelly, [[john-palfrey|John Palfrey]], and later
Yochai Benkler and Hal Roberts — tracing a single argument through two very different
decades. Phase one (2007–2013) is **networked-protest optimism**: mapping the Persian,
Arabic, and Russian blogospheres as spaces where dissent could organize outside
state media, reading Ukraine's Orange Revolution, Burma's Saffron Revolution, and
Kenya's 2007–08 crisis as early cases of networked political mobilization, and — in
its clearest US-policy application — quantifying how the networked public sphere
shaped the 2012 SOPA-PIPA fight and the net-neutrality debate. Phase two (2017–2020)
inverts the frame entirely: the same measurement toolkit (media-cloud link analysis,
network mapping) is turned on **America's own media ecosystem**, and "Partisanship,
Propaganda, and Disinformation" and its book-length sequel *Network Propaganda*
conclude that 2016-era disinformation was less a Russian-bot problem than a
structural one — an "asymmetric" US media ecosystem in which the right-wing press
insulates itself from fact-checking. The same team's *Mail-In Voter Fraud* (2020)
applies the method to the Trump-era vote-fraud narrative. Directly continues into
[[misinformation-and-elections]] and cross-cuts [[digital-colonialism-and-global-south]]
in its Global South case studies.
