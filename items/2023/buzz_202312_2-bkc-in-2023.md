2023 in review
In this final BKC Buzz of 2023 we highlight selected work of the Berkman Klein Center’s fellows, visiting scholars, faculty associates, affiliates, directors, students, and staff. (For a fuller picture, visit the Community (https://cyber.harvard.edu/community) and Events (https://cyber.harvard.edu/events) sections of the BKC website.)
This September, the BKC community – past, present, near, far – gathered to celebrate 25 years (!) of teaching, research, and building. The two-day, 25th anniversary reunion was a supercollider of debates, keynotes, planning, fellowship, and fun.

Apropos the community 📣 a reminder that currently there are open opportunities for joining BKC, including our annual Open Call for Fellowship Applications (https://cyber.harvard.edu/getinvolved/fellowships/2425Fellows) and the Institute for Rebooting Social Media’s Call for 2024-2025 RSM Visiting Scholars (https://rebootingsocialmedia.org/2023/11/21/call-for-2024-2025-rsm-visiting-scholars/) .
https://asml.cyber.harvard.edu/

BKC’s ambitious new Applied Social Media Lab is also accepting applications for multiple roles (https://asml.cyber.harvard.edu/#apply) . The Lab aims to bridge research and practice and give industry-trained technologists the freedom to build social media solutions that center the public interest. Growing out of BKC’s Institute for Rebooting Social Media, the Lab launched in October with a daylong Future of the Internet Summit (https://asml.cyber.harvard.edu/summit/) featuring leading thinkers like Larry Lessig, who spoke with The Verge about the Lab and why AI and social media are causing a free speech crisis (https://www.theverge.com/23929233/lawrence-lessig-free-speech-first-amendment-ai-content-moderation-decoder-interview) for the internet.

Indeed. It’s an AI, AI, AI, AI [DEL: world :DEL] year.

The existing and potential impacts of generative AI across a range of domains fixated the public – and regulators – after the rollout of ChatGPT and similar systems. The BKC community expanded its engagement with artificial intelligence research and regulatory schemes to encompass new questions raised by the growing use of generative AI.

In May, BKC hosted Co-Designing Generative Futures (https://cyber.harvard.edu/workshops/gai2023/) , a workshop that kicked off a collaborative global effort to build a knowledge network of experts, learners, and decisionmakers, jointly led by BKC, the NCIS at BI Norwegian Business School, ITS Rio, and the TUM Think Tank, in partnership with the Global Network of Internet & Society Centers. Among other outputs, the workshop prompted a set of multidisciplinary, transnational reflections about generative AI (https://medium.com/berkman-klein-center/generative-futures/home) from Samson Esayas, Alexa Hasse, Sameer Hinduja, Petra Molnar, Bill Shribman, and Elisabeth Sylvan. The Co-Designing Generative Futures workshop was preceded by a series of conversations moderated by Sue Hendrickson and Rumman Chowdhury that tackled •accountable (https://cyber.harvard.edu/events/how-generative-ai-changing-landscape-ai-harms) •technical
(https://cyber.harvard.edu/events/balancing-transparency-and-security-open-research-generative-ai) •oversight (https://cyber.harvard.edu/events/enabling-accountable-technical-oversight-generative-ai) of generative AI.

https://aipedagogy.org/

Related new projects this year include the Initiative on Artificial Intelligence and Law (https://cyber.harvard.edu/story/2023-07/hls-bkc-new-initiative-artificial-intelligence-and-law) , directed by Oren-Bar Gill and Cass Sunstein, and metaLAB (at) Harvard’s AI Pedagogy Project (https://aipedagogy.org/) , a dynamic resource aimed at guiding educators in critically engaging with AI tools in their teaching. (Much more to come from both in 2024.)
The Institute for Rebooting Social Media (RSM), BKC’s largest in-house project, was a juggernaut of activities, farewelling assembly fellows (https://rebootingsocialmedia.org/fellows-summary/) , welcoming visiting scholars (https://rebootingsocialmedia.org/2023/05/02/2023-24-visiting-scholars/) , and hosting a score of events, not least Now & Next: Platform Accountability and Content Governance (https://rebootingsocialmedia.org/events/now-next-platform-accountability-and-content-governance/) with the Oversight Board, as well as a series of guest speakers (https://rebootingsocialmedia.org/2023/09/27/rsm-fall-speaker-series/) that wrapped up last week with Ethan Zuckerman (https://rebootingsocialmedia.org/events/the-future-of-social-media-research-rsm-speaker-series/) on the future of social media research. RSM fellows and visiting scholars – and directors – participated in a range of conversations at the center of today’s urgent social media issues. Jonathan Zittrain spoke with the New York
Times about shadowbanning (https://www.nytimes.com/interactive/2023/01/13/business/what-is-shadow-banning.html) and about the appeal of alternative social networks (https://www.nytimes.com/2023/04/19/technology/personaltech/tiktok-twitter-facebook-social.html) (with a shoutout to Ben Grosser’s Minus (https://minus.social/) experiment). Yong Jin Park and Jeong-Nam Kim debunked the notion that more data increases the quality of AI personalization (https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/10776990221129245?journalCode=jmqc) . Nadah Feteih and Elodie Vialle argued that companies should involve at-risk communities in the design process (https://techpolicy.press/centering-community-voices-how-tech-companies-can-better-engage-with-civil-society-organizations/) of new technologies. Jabari Evans talked with Meredith Clark about media, journalism, and Black community (https://open.spotify.com/episode/0rj6KTjihJFekecBNlp8hx?si=yRKUzOVjSEKDjgGp8qYtIw&nd=1) . In the New York Times Kate Klonick
argued that the trend of using state authorities and cherry-picked federal forums (https://www.nytimes.com/2023/07/13/opinion/federal-judge-biden-social-media.html) to decide issues of national internet regulation is concerning. Joe Bak-Coleman wrote about the complicated nature of TikTok's API guidelines for researchers (https://techpolicy.press/tiktoks-api-guidelines-are-a-minefield-for-researchers/) . Joseph Walther explained the social approval theory of online hate (https://theconversation.com/online-likes-for-toxic-social-media-posts-prompt-more-and-more-hateful-messages-218220) (apropos, in Science, Aaron Shaw also published on online extremism (https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/sciadv.adk2031) this year). Swati Srivastava made a case for international relations researchers to become more involved in the global research on algorithmic governance
(https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/perspectives-on-politics/article/algorithmic-governance-and-the-international-politics-of-big-tech/3C04908735A5F2EE8A70AFED647741FB) . For more, visit the RSM website (https://rebootingsocialmedia.org/) .
https://rebootingsocialmedia.org/
Beyond RSM, Jessica Fjeld and collaborators dug into the relationships between generative AI and the arts (https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/science.adh4451) and •intellectual (https://medium.com/berkman-klein-center/devalue-or-defend-generative-ai-artists-and-the-law-7b5f514ff822) •property (https://theconversation.com/generative-ai-is-a-minefield-for-copyright-law-207473) . Ruth Okediji and Rebecca Tushnet also addressed interactions between AI and IP, most recently for a Rappaport Forum (https://hls.harvard.edu/today/intellectual-property-experts-discuss-fair-use-in-the-age-of-ai/) and the Hard Fork podcast (https://www.nytimes.com/2023/11/03/podcasts/hard-fork-executive-order-ai-copyright.html) , respectively. Relatedly, Florian Martin-Bariteau moderated a discussion of copyright and AI training (https://youtu.be/9CSB0oG2KTw?si=cf6n8SJ50X2mTC4h) with Ruth Okediji, Faith Majekolagbe, and Michael Geist. Gregory Gondwe interviewed journalists in Sub-Saharan Africa
(https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=4392918) about using ChatGPT. Kim Albrecht asked ChatGPT to categorize its knowledge (https://www.harvardmagazine.com/2023/12/mapping-chat-gpt-metalab)  and mapped the results. Juliana Castro Varon spoke with Elizabeth Dubois about the past, present, and (generative AI-inflected) future of image manipulation (https://www.polcommtech.com/post/image-manipulation-juliana-castro-varon) . AI was a major sticking point in the Hollywood strikes; David Craig and Stuart Cunningham contended that the online creator economy (https://theconversation.com/with-the-end-of-the-hollywood-writers-and-actors-strikes-the-creator-economy-is-the-next-frontier-for-organized-labor-217657) broadly is the next arena of labor organizing.
https://cyber.harvard.edu/events/7-fellows-predict-future-many-questions-and-some-answers-human-capital-artificial

Individually and together, Bruce Schneier and Nathan Sanders published a thick bundle of pieces about the effects of generative AI on various aspects of political life, e.g., campaigning (https://ash.harvard.edu/who%E2%80%99s-accountable-ai-usage-digital-campaign-ads-right-now-no-one) , disinformation (https://theconversation.com/ai-disinformation-is-a-threat-to-elections-learning-to-spot-russian-chinese-and-iranian-meddling-in-other-countries-can-help-the-us-prepare-for-2024-214358) , and lobbying (https://www.nytimes.com/2023/01/15/opinion/ai-chatgpt-lobbying-democracy.html) . With Henry Farrell, they argued for public investment (https://slate.com/technology/2023/04/ai-public-option.html) in AI development. Elizabeth Dubois facilitated a lively discussion on how AI is being deployed in Canadian political contexts (https://www.polcommtech.com/post/the-use-of-ai-in-canadian-politics) . Aviv Ovadya explained the need for more robust testing
(https://www.marketplace.org/shows/marketplace-tech/gpt-4-needs-more-robust-testing-red-team-member-says/) of GPT-4, and Sahar Massachi joined the Integrity Institute podcast for a conversation with OpenAI about trust and safety (https://integrityinstitute.org/podcast/trust-in-tech-e19-eldritch-open-ai-gpt) . Desmond Patton, Aviv Landau, and Siva Mathiyazhagan explored how, ethically, to use ChatGPT in social work (https://www.journals.uchicago.edu/doi/epdf/10.1086/726042) . Kendra Albert balked at using LLMs in legal practice (https://peertube.dair-institute.org/w/o6sb7f7RwapWBJd9VC61t4) . And among her •many (https://www.wired.com/story/ai-desperately-needs-global-oversight/) •engagements (https://www.theguardian.com/technology/2023/may/29/rumman-chowdhury-interview-artificial-intelligence-accountability) •around (https://www.kqed.org/forum/2010101893064/artificial-intelligence-is-booming-how-should-it-be-regulated) •responsible AI
(https://thehill.com/opinion/technology/4047323-artificial-intelligence-doesnt-have-to-be-inhumane/) , Rumman Chowdhury explored technology-facilitated gender-based violence (https://unesdoc.unesco.org/ark:/48223/pf0000387483/PDF/387483eng.pdf.multi) in an era of generative AI in a UNESCO report.

The Initiative for a Representative First Amendment (IfRFA) hosted a panel on “Weaponizing Misogyny”: Gender-Based Harassment’s Impact on Journalists and Free Expression (https://cyber.harvard.edu/events/weaponizing-misogyny-gender-based-harassments-impact-journalists-and-free-expression) , and Pratika Katiyar continued the discussion of harassment and press freedom (https://medium.com/berkman-klein-center/online-harassment-threat-to-world-press-freedom-540adb768c54) in an interview with Elodie Vialle. IfRFA also hosted The Afterlife of Anthony Comstock: Abortion, Bodily Autonomy, and Obscenity (1873 - 2023) (https://cyber.harvard.edu/events/afterlife-anthony-comstock) , a panel in preparation for ComstockCon (https://www.comstockcon.com/) , coming this spring.
Continuing BKC’s commitment to scholarship with impact, community members advised, counseled, and commented on the current abundance of regulatory efforts and proposals, particularly around artificial intelligence.

Elisabeth Sylvan and Armando Guio Español proposed measures for governments in Latin America to consider (https://medium.com/berkman-klein-center/generative-ai-what-should-governments-in-latin-america-do-9ca8a1f73051) when navigating regional responses to the risks of generative AI. Maroussia Lévesque, Tom Zick, Dennis Redeker, You Jeen Ha, Florian Martin-Bariteau, and Mason Kortz explored putting the principles of the White House’s Blueprint for an AI Bill of Rights (https://medium.com/berkman-klein-center/building-on-the-blueprint-9471276f2cce) into practice, while Russell Newman, Nick Couldry, Velislava Hillman, Mitzi László and Gregory Narr discussed the comments to the US Federal Trade Commission's Advanced Notice of Proposed Rulemaking on commercial surveillance (https://jacobin.com/2023/09/federal-trade-commission-ai-data-collection-privacy-anprm-commercial-surveillance) . Salomé Viljoen, Ifeoma Ajunwa, and Elettra Bietti provided initial reactions to President Biden's Executive
Order on Artificial Intelligence (https://lpeproject.org/blog/seven-reactions-to-bidens-executive-order-on-artificial-intelligence/) . Jasmine McNealy explained why rural communities must be included (https://www.techpolicy.press/we-need-a-policy-agenda-for-rural-ai/) in the development of policies related to AI.

https://youtu.be/USfr2GxOVHg?si=uifGphFN_GfcXfVn

Jonathan Zittrain interviewed Kashmir Hill, author of Your Face Belongs to Us (https://youtu.be/USfr2GxOVHg?si=uifGphFN_GfcXfVn) , about the facial recognition company Clearview AI. “Failing to deal with this company’s behavior when it started is the biggest public policy failure in the digital space in a generation,” Zittrain had previously written elsewhere (https://twitter.com/zittrain/status/1494122166803124231) .
Noha Lea Halim and Urs Gasser juxtaposed the US Algorithmic Accountability Act and the EU AI Act (https://deliverypdf.ssrn.com/delivery.php?ID=586024090004110064094097023117114105036053067045062087091090095119013071085097088104033016012031048048013086080030099091112019009094094009064093080016112095117093127055062048111127125084102127070000091095026085088010069003018102083029007001110018098101&EXT=pdf&INDEX=TRUE) and offered some reflections on what the commonalities and differences might teach us about the future direction of AI governance. Anupam Chander argued that authoritarians might exploit mechanisms in the EU's Digital Services Act (https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=4606282) . Maroussia Lévesque and Florian Martin-Bariteau unpacked Canada’s proposed Artificial Intelligence and Data Act (https://www.cigionline.org/articles/ai-regulation-should-not-be-a-blank-cheque-for-government/) . Bulelani Jili explained why Kenya needs to update its regulatory framework
(https://techpolicy.press/kenya-must-update-its-regulatory-frameworks-to-keep-pace-with-ai/) in light of AI development. And Petra Molnar spoke about the EU's AI Act and its potential implications in the border surveillance space (https://www.codastory.com/authoritarian-tech/petra-molnar-ai-act/) . Also, with the United Nations Human Rights Office, she and Lorna McGregor launched a study advocating for a human-rights based approach to digital border technologies (https://www.ohchr.org/en/documents/tools-and-resources/digital-border-governance-human-rights-based-approach) .

Finally, the BKC Youth and Media team engaged young people on conceptions of well-being; their report "provides a methodology that academics, governments and nonprofits across the world can use to replicate this research and learn about young people’s understanding of well-being (https://cyber.harvard.edu/publication/2022/transforming-state-art-offline-approaches-digital-world) in their own regions." Separately, Youth and Media lead Sandra Cortesi wrote on the importance of engaging young people (https://cyber.harvard.edu/publication/2022/15-ways-engage-youth-within-your-company-and-why-you-should-do-it) in business and government settings. As artificial intelligence systems – and regulations – are deployed, youth voices are doubly important.

The team at BKC wishes you a peaceful and restorative winter break. Thanks to all of our community members for their generous, creative, and bold contributions this year. We look forward to being together again soon.
