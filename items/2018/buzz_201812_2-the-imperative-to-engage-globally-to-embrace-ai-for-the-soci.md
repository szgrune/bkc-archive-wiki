In 2017, the Berkman Klein Center and the MIT Media Lab launched the Ethics and Governance of Artificial Intelligence Initiative (https://cyber.harvard.edu/topics/ethics-and-governance-ai) . The overall aim of this Initiative is to bolster the use of AI for the public good through evidence-based research and capacity building.

We are excited to share some of our recent work on AI at the international level with a focus on global governance and inclusion (https://cyber.harvard.edu/projects/ai-global-governance-and-inclusion) . Our work on this track, in collaboration with partner institutions around the world, began with a global dialogue series with policymakers and stakeholders across many sectors and regions to get a better grasp on the challenges and opportunities of AI governance in their respective social and cultural contexts and from diverse perspectives.

During this listening tour, a number of key challenges to promoting AI for the social good emerged. We are excited to share here some of the key learnings as well as some of the approaches and tools that we have developed to begin addressing those challenges. By sharing some of our ongoing work and resources, we hope to highlight the importance of building a more inclusive AI ecosystem at a global level, and invite additional engagement and collaboration across stakeholders.

A major takeaway from the past year of engaging with stakeholders working on AI globally is that while the promise of AI-based technologies is enormous, the potential risks are equally if not more staggering; many participants in the listening tour emphasized the concern that AI currently primarily benefis typically advantaged groups, often at the expense of others. These benefit gaps are furthered when the primary developers, deployers, and decision-makers involved in AI are not representative of the populations they impact.

In response, we’ve made a key part of our ethics and governance of AI work centering inclusivity and internationalization via a sustained, strategic, and networked approach. This began with a 2017 Global Symposium on AI and Inclusion (https://aiandinclusion.org/) , which brought together over 170 participants from more than 40 countries and has informed a number of initiatives that set the stage for building inclusion into researching AI and its impacts.

We developed a corresponding research roadmap (https://drive.google.com/file/d/1kLT-TGBvJgPzcOcIh0VeCbl3Emt7BWkl/view?usp=sharing) for those working at the intersection of AI and policy to use as a framework for prioritizing inclusion in research on AI across the stages of technical development. The roadmap and findings from the symposium not only influenced our own research agenda, but served as key inputs for other entities. The International Development Research Centre (IDRC) used our resources as key inputs for their AI and Human Development Report (https://idl-bnc-idrc.dspacedirect.org/handle/10625/56949) , which continues to influence Canada’s AI strategy and is spurring a forthcoming AI and Development Fund (https://www.idrc.ca/en/stories/artificial-intelligence-and-human-development) for the Global South.

We've also brought our research roadmap to life to promote inclusive AI. One such effort involved creating open access youth-oriented learning materials (https://dlrp.berkman.harvard.edu/tools/search?tid=9&tid_1=57&tid_3=All) about relevant areas of youth life associated with the digital world, which have reached over 350,000 youth and educators from India to Nigeria.

At the global level, there are two key information asymmetries at play: an asymmetry of knowledge and resources between those developing AI and those using it (or impacted by its use), and an asymmetry of access to AI-based technologies and their benefits. These asymmetries are stratified primarily across the Global North and Global South, where completely different conversations about AI are often taking place among research centers, NGOs, and the public given the discrepancy in access to AI and resources for understanding AI’s impact.

This asymmetry is a barrier for innovative work on ethical AI for regions with fewer resources and inhibits a truly global understanding of AI’s global impact. Bridging these asymmetries can help promote more globally inclusive AI as well as a greater foresight of global risks.

Drawing from our long-standing commitment to global capacity building, and with an emphasis on building up capacity in the Global South to mitigate information asymmetries, we played a key role in incubating new centers working on the ethical and regulatory implications of AI. For example, in the summer of 2018, we helped to support the launch of the Singapore Management University (SMU) School of Law’s Center for AI & Data Governance (https://caidg.smu.edu.sg/) , and the new branch of the Digital Asia Hub (DAH) (https://www.digitalasiahub.org/2017/07/26/asia-pacific-regional-igf-forum-bangkok-2017/) in Bangkok as part of the expanding Global Network of Internet & Society Centers (https://networkofcenters.net/) . Both centers will serve as vital junctions in a growing regional and international network of institutions working on the ethics and governance of AI in the Global South

Bridge information gaps (https://medium.com/@BKCHarvard/a83521ddae76#c42a)

Policymakers face difficult problems and choices when addressing AI systems. A lack of sound AI impact metrics, the complexity of AI, and the rapid speed of its development, all make answering the questions of cost and benefit, and scale of impact, challenging to answer.

To help policymakers clear away the hype and uncertainty surrounding AI technologies, and learn where and how policy can be most useful, we are playing the role of a bridge builder. In this role, we are focused on offering concrete steps, frameworks, and tools to help inform policymakers in making practical, evidence-based decisions at both regional and international levels.

Upon the invitation of German Chancellor Angela Merkel, for instance, we engaged with the German Federal Digital Council (https://www.bundeskanzlerin.de/bkin-en/news/the-faces-behind-the-digital-council-1504894) , on which Berkman Klein Center Executive Director Urs Gasser serves as a member. The AI global governance and inclusion team, supported by NoC experts, recently reviewed Germany’s provisional Keypoints AI Document (https://www.bmwi.de/Redaktion/EN/Pressemitteilungen/2018/20180718-key-points-for-federal-government-strategy-on-artificial-intelligence.html) , which outlined the main elements of the German national AI strategy, and provided extensive feedback to the Council. Many of the inputs have been incorporated in the recently finalized and announced (https://www.bundesregierung.de/breg-de/suche/strategie-kuenstliche-intelligenz-der-bundesregierung-1551264) German AI strategy.

Also at the national level, our work with the Canadian government is helping Canada and other governing bodies to think through and identify how existing human rights law frameworks can provide helpful guidance for policy on AI (https://cyber.harvard.edu/publication/2018/artificial-intelligence-human-rights) .

At the international level, the United Nations is developing an action plan for the UN High-Level Committee on Programmes (HLCP) based off of a first draft we developed, which will guide all UN agencies on the deployment of AI internally and support member states in advancing their use of AI. This effort follows work conducted with the UN International Telecommunications Union (ITU) on a module that helps set the stage for AI governance (https://www.itu.int/en/ITU-D/Conferences/GSR/Documents/GSR2018/documents/AISeries_GovernanceModule_GSR18.pdf) .
