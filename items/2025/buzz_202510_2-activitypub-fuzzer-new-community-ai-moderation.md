# ActivityPub Fuzzer; new community; AI moderation

The Applied Social Media Lab recently launched the ActivityPub Fuzzer, a tool aimed at enhancing interoperability between social media platforms. ASML Senior Engineer Darius Kazemi explained that interoperability:

“is really about giving people the choice of where they want their digital homes to be, and not siloing people from one another just based on where they happen to choose those homes.”

Enter: the ActivityPub Fuzzer, developed by Kazemi. The ActivityPub Fuzzer is an open-source program that allows developers to build social media platforms with the ActivityPub protocol. By emulating data from across the Fediverse, the Fuzzer gives developers insight into how to make their own networks compatible with existing services, ultimately enabling users to follow content from multiple services without creating multiple accounts.

Read more about the ActivityPub Fuzzer on the ASML website (https://asml.cyber.harvard.edu/2025/10/01/introducing-the-asml-activitypub-fuzzer-improving-testing-in-the-fediverse/) . And watch Darius explain the tool here (https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=EGo7ZaBG-4s) .

** Automating Content Policy
------------------------------------------------------------

Yesterday BKC welcomed Zentropi Co-Founder Dave Willner for a conversation with ASML Senior Director Meg Marco about how AI can be used to moderate content on social media platforms (https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=XKJWUCliqpM) and beyond, reducing the mental toll moderation takes on humans. Willner introduced the audience to CoPE, an open-source small language model that classifies content based on customizable specifications, i.e. specific policies.

Audience members wanted to know how the model handles the tricky line between moderation and censorship and how it interprets the cultural relevance of certain content even if it technically violates specific policies (citing Nick Ut’s 1973 Pulitzer Prize-winning photo “Napalm Girl”). Willner explained that the model’s size and accessibility allows organizations to more easily build in labeling that aligns with a specific platform’s cultural norms.

While Zentropi represents his most recent effort, Willner has been at the forefront of AI in content moderation for years, with stints at OpenAI and Meta (then Facebook). In 2024, he visited the Institute for Rebooting Social Media to share his thoughts on how and why AI should be used to regulate content on social media platforms. You can watch that earlier discussion (https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=hCoyK4Zsnm4&list=PL68azUN8PTNiGf1DWcv7VFeGnCJqLp6KU&index=7) on BKC’s YouTube channel, along with all of BKC’s past events (https://www.youtube.com/@BKCHarvard/videos) .

** More from the BKC Community
------------------------------------------------------------

In an article for AI & Society, Faculty Associate David Nemer (https://cyber.harvard.edu/people/david-nemer) and André Sobral work to disrupt the narrative (https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00146-025-02664-5) that autonomous AI is independently intelligent and self-sufficient, arguing that AI is embedded in—and dependent on—human infrastructure. “The labor of ghost workers, crowdworkers, and content moderators does not merely supplement automation; it animates it. It gives so-called intelligent systems their meaning, their data, and their utility.”

In Tech Policy Press, Faculty Associate Jasmine McNealy (https://cyber.harvard.edu/people/jasmine-mcnealy) and Yewande O. Addie outline their study on the use of a chatbot called S.A.R.A.H. (https://www.techpolicy.press/lessons-from-nigeria-and-kenya-on-digital-colonialism-in-ai-health-messaging/) (Smart AI Resource Assistant for Health) in Kenya and Nigeria. The study observed the chatbot’s medical precision but lack of cultural insight, highlighting how “generative AI risks entrenching patterns of exploitation that echo older hierarchies of power.”

Affiliate Bruce Schneier (https://cyber.harvard.edu/people/bschneier) coauthors an opinion piece for CSO on the dangers of AI cyberattacks (https://www.csoonline.com/article/4069075/autonomous-ai-hacking-and-the-future-of-cybersecurity.html) and the potential to use AI as a means of cybersecurity; the piece outlines several possible outcomes of integrating LLMs into cyber defense. “We don’t know how the different technologies of cyber-offense and cyber-defense will be amenable to AI enhancement, but we can extrapolate a possible series of overlapping developments.”

Schneier and Affiliate Nathan Sanders (https://cyber.harvard.edu/people/nathan-sanders) have also published a new book, Rewiring Democracy: How AI Will Transform Our Politics, Government, and Citizenship (https://mitpress.mit.edu/9780262049948/rewiring-democracy/) . The book outlines ways AI is altering how we practice democracy and explores both the potential pitfalls and opportunities.

Affiliate Rudy Fraser (https://cyber.harvard.edu/people/rudy-fraser) is quoted in a TechCrunch article on the increasing decentralization of social media (https://techcrunch.com/2025/10/16/a-new-wave-of-social-media-apps-provide-hope-in-a-doomscrolling-world/) , which features a slate of new platforms—including Fraser’s Blacksky—aimed at giving users more control over their content and audiences. “Sometimes you need a global stage. Sometimes you just want a cozy corner with close internet friends where you can control who sees what.”

The Harvard Gazette interviews (https://news.harvard.edu/gazette/story/2025/10/over-60-and-online/) former BKC Executive Director John Palfrey on his new book Wired Wisdom: How to Age Better Online (https://press.uchicago.edu/ucp/books/book/chicago/W/bo248650003.html) , cowritten with BKC alum Eszter Hargittai, which contends with how older adults think about issues like privacy and connection online. “If we treat older adults like they don’t know anything, that’s not a very good starting point for figuring out the right interventions, whether it’s changing the design of the technology, creating new laws, or intervening as family members.”

For more, visit the BKC website’s Community (https://cyber.harvard.edu/community) section, where writing (and more) from fellows, faculty associates, affiliates, and staff is aggregated.
