** Inside the Black Box & Shaping Superintelligence
------------------------------------------------------------

“I think my overall takeaway is that there is no one silver bullet here. There is not one secret solution that we should turn to and rely on.”

Ashley Deeks, former Deputy Legal Advisor for the White House’s National Security Council and Vice Dean of the University of Virginia’s School of Law, joined BKC last week to discuss The Double Black Box: National Security, Artificial Intelligence, and the Struggle for Democratic Accountability (https://global.oup.com/academic/product/the-double-black-box-9780197520901?cc=us〈=en&) . Her discussion with Harvard Law School Professor Kristen Eichensehr explored questions and challenges around transparency when AI, an operationally opaque technology to begin with, is deployed in national security processes often shrouded in secrecy—creating a “double black box” effect that makes proper governance and accountability difficult.

In addition to outlining some of the traditional systems that already exist to keep national security processes in check, the conversation also highlighted some of the differences in how the Biden Administration and Trump Administration have approached AI governance.

Watch the full event here (https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=bNHyOvYGQzg) .
https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=N7OvAE-Hze0

“Rather than thinking about super intelligence as a big machine, I want us to instead think about it like… a partially, maybe eventually increasingly self-aware collective intelligence.”

E. Glen Weyl closed out our Fall Speaker Series with a sweeping argument for reimagining superintelligence not as a single, autonomous machine working in competition with humans, but as a collective sociotechnical system built from people, institutions, and AI working in concert.

He traced how concepts from sociology and game theory, such as common knowledge, can be translated into actionable design principles for more democratic digital systems. Highlighting emerging civic models from Taiwan and Japan, he described how platforms that visualize points of consensus and disagreement can counter polarization and foster what he calls prosocial media.

Moderator and BKC Faculty Associate Moira Weigel drew on media theory and sociology to ask how communities are formed, how they define outsiders, and how the architecture of different media shapes collective life. Together, they explored how cross-cutting identities might help rebuild trust, reduce polarization, and make our digital spaces more genuinely democratic.
