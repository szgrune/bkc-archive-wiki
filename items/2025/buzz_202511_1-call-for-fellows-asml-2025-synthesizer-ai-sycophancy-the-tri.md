** Friend, Flatterer, or Foe? The Psychology & Liability of Chatbots
------------------------------------------------------------

What happens when chatbots affirm our darkest impulses? And when harm occurs, who should be held responsible?   Last month, BKC welcomed author and New York Times journalist Kashmir Hill for a conversation with BKC Senior Advisor Jordi Weinstock and ASML Senior Director Meg Marco on AI sycophancy. The conversation explored some of the documented psychological harms that have already occurred, including the recent case of 16-year-old Adam Raine (https://www.nytimes.com/2025/08/26/technology/chatgpt-openai-suicide.html?unlocked_article_code=1.hE8.T-3v.bPoDlWD8z5vo&smid=url-share) , who died by suicide within months of confiding to ChatGPT about his mental health experiences. Hill expressed her concern about just how impactful this technology has been over the last couple of years: “I do feel like we are in this moment where we are doing an unprecedented, global psychological experiment on human beings.” Weinstock argued that companies that create chatbots ought to be held accountable for
harms in the same way that a company that produces physical products would be.

You can watch the full event here (https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=0YlQcg2-xzg) . ** Inside the AI Triad
------------------------------------------------------------

With nearly 100 in-person attendees and over 130 online viewers, three different worldviews about AI collided and converged at BKC on Wednesday at an event to examine competing visions for AI's future, which uncovered surprising areas of overlap. Jason Crawford from the Roots of Progress Institute emphasized individual agency in shaping our relationship to technology, while Brian McGrail of the Center for AI Safety Action Fund stressed that AI's speed of development—potentially “faster by orders of magnitude” than the industrial revolution—demands urgent intervention to prevent the worst outcomes from materializing. Amba Kak of the AI Now Institute reframed the debate entirely, arguing that discussions of AI's transformational potential obscure its profit-driven research incentives and increasing market concentration in the industry.

Moderated by Faculty Director Jonathan Zittrain, the panel expressed concern about the current pace of private AI development and the destabilizing race to deploy it into society..The event was part of BKC's broader initiative on the AI “Triad” to foster dialogue across divided intellectual and practitioner communities and surface the moral, technological, and empirical premises driving the AI conversation.

Watch the recording of the event here (https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=kGa0ZPFsRuo&t=517s) . And just a reminder: you can rewatch all past Speaker Series events on the BKC YouTube channel (https://www.youtube.com/c/bkcharvard) !

** More from the BKC Community
------------------------------------------------------------

Affiliate Backtosch Mustafa coauthors this article for HealthAffairs on the potential benefits of agentic AI in medicine (https://www.healthaffairs.org/content/forefront/could-agentic-ai-improve-access-maternal-health-care) —particularly in maternal healthcare. “In areas of the US struggling with maternal health deserts, AI agents might help expand capacity and assist patients in a variety of ways.”

Affiliate Joe Bak-Coleman and his coauthors explore the unique challenges researchers in the technology and social media space can face (https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.19894) due to potential industry influence on data. “Moreover, technology companies historically have been, and continue to be, a major source of funding for this field. These asymmetries in information and funding raise significant concerns about the potential for undue industry influence on the scientific record.”

In an op-ed for The Guardian, Affiliate Maroussia Lévesque and her coauthor argue against the narrative that the US has moved away from regulating AI development (https://www.theguardian.com/commentisfree/2025/oct/23/us-artificial-intelligence-regulations) . “The US isn’t deregulating AI – it’s regulating where most people aren’t looking. Beneath the free-market rhetoric, Washington actually intervenes to control the building blocks of AI systems.”

In Prospect, Faculty Associate Ethan Zuckerman writes on the importance of supporting public service journalism (https://www.prospectmagazine.co.uk/ideas/technology/ai/71464/trump-chatbots-deepfakes-eroding-shared-reality) as AI increasingly disrupts our sense of a shared reality. “Reporting and fact-checking—our best tools for anchoring debate in a shared reality—must continue to be financially viable and available.”

Faculty Associate Mailyn Fidler argues in Lawfare that the proposed UN Cybercrime Convention functions as a global mutual legal assistance treaty (https://www.lawfaremedia.org/article/procedure-as-substance-in-the-un-cybercrime-convention) for all crimes: “the UN Convention carries stakes for signing that are much higher than mere cooperation on cybercrime. The stakes become setting foundational rules for cooperating on all crime—indeed, changing those foundational rules—a significantly more expansive remit.”

Affiliate and Harvard Library Innovation Lab Director Jack Cushman joins the Pioneers and Pathfinders podcast to discuss the role of libraries in the digital age, and how lawyers can best prepare themselves to understand and operate in an AI-driven world (https://www.seyfarth.com/news-insights/pioneers-and-pathfinders-jack-cushman.html) , even without coding skills. “... In the AI era, they need to be able to think about the consequences of these tools, and that's very different from being able to implement them yourself.”

For more, visit the BKC website’s Community (https://cyber.harvard.edu/community) section, where writing (and more) from fellows, faculty associates, affiliates, and staff is aggregated.
