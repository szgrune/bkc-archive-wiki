** Uncertain Models
------------------------------------------------------------

Last week, MIT’s Jacob Andreas joined BKC Chief AI Scientist Josh Joseph (https://cyber.harvard.edu/people/joshua-joseph) for a conversation on what it would take to build language models that can not only model the world—but also model themselves. Andreas traced how today’s systems can fail twice: first when they get a fact wrong and again when they confidently believe it. He outlined emerging approaches to improve internal coherence and help models better assess their own confidence, while underscoring that technical fixes alone aren’t enough. To build more reliable systems, he argued, we first need to decide what kinds of reasoning and uncertainty we actually want them to display.

The discussion turned to personalization—how models should behave toward users when they encounter inconsistencies or conflicting values. Should they ask clarifying questions? Come with an instruction manual? “I think we're just starting to see people train these models to actually, you know, in a way that's sensitive to the context in which interaction is going to take place,” Andreas noted.

“It feels so dependent on the specific user—how they wish to be navigated through that,” he observed. But do users always know what’s best for them? As Andreas put it, “There's a lot of conversation right now about a sort of psychosis induced by interactions with these models directly as a result of these sorts of personalization behaviors. And, again, I think when you're inside one of those interactions, of course you're going to say, “Keep giving me exactly what I'm asking for.”

The conversation probed the murky space where personalization in model behavior meets its policy and psychological implications. Watch the full talk on BKC’s YouTube channel (https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=gMIo9AzwjyY) .

Community Q&A: Fellow Sean McGregor

Incoming BKC Fellow Sean McGregor (https://cyber.harvard.edu/people/sean-mcgregor) brings a deep understanding and years of experience in the artificial intelligence space, with a focus on safety, ethics, and assessment. Learn more about his work, and stay tuned for future features of other BKC community members here in the Buzz.

Which community/communities do you hope to most impact with your work?
Humanity! More specifically: frontier model audit, frontier model evaluation, AI safety data (paging the librarians!), and insurers.

What’s something about your work that you think would surprise people?
Building more powerful AI systems is in direct conflict with the methods required to understand AI risk. Developers will always choose to use risk evaluations to improve the system rather than measure it — unless there is an outside force (customers, insurance, etc.) demanding truly independent information.

Why is your work important right now?
Risk from AI systems is scaling far faster than our capacity to understand and manage it.

If an insurer asked you for the top three signals that predict lower AI risk, what would you pick?
1. Can you identify a responsible party bearing most/all the risks?
2. Is the responsible party deeply knowledgeable about the task and technology?
3. Does that knowledgeable, responsible party not want insurance? Then it is low risk. :)

If you look five years ahead, what do you hope will have changed about how society measures and evaluates AI systems?
There absolutely must be a robust, independent safety assessment ecosystem with technical and application expertise that generates reliable information about the risks of AI systems. With a mature ecosystem of insurers, auditors, rating agencies, and other forms of evaluation, society will be on a much better path.

** More from the BKC Community
------------------------------------------------------------

Faculty Associate George Chaloub (https://cyber.harvard.edu/people/george-chalhoub) writes in Times Higher Education that clear guidelines and regulation must be established in order to preserve the integrity of academic journals (https://www.timeshighereducation.com/opinion/we-must-set-rules-ai-use-scientific-writing-and-peer-review) —both from writers and reviewers. Some of his suggestions include requiring open declarations of any AI use and training referees on identifying hidden prompts embedded in manuscripts.

Faculty Associate Mark Esposito (https://cyber.harvard.edu/people/mark-esposito) coauthors this piece for the World Economic Forum arguing that AI-facilitated recruitment methods (https://www.weforum.org/stories/2025/09/ai-powered-recruitment-inclusion-transparency/) can cultivate a human-centered approach that prioritizes a candidate’s capability over credentials, so long as there are adequate guardrails in place for how the technology is designed and used.

Following President Trump’s executive order advancing a deal to keep TikTok operating in the US, Faculty Associate Anupam Chander (https://cyber.harvard.edu/people/anupam-chander) is quoted in The Washington Post on how the structure of the deal essentially undermines the original purpose (https://www.washingtonpost.com/technology/2025/09/25/trump-order-tiktok-deal/) of the divest-or-ban order signed by President Biden in 2024: “By shifting the app to more friendly, politically aligned owners, my concern is that we’ve introduced a different kind of propaganda.”

Affiliates Nathan Sanders (https://cyber.harvard.edu/people/nathan-sanders) and Bruce Schneier (https://cyber.harvard.edu/people/bschneier) write for TIME speculating on the range of effects AI could have on the 2026 U.S. midterm election (https://time.com/7321098/ai-2026-midterm-elections/) , including contributing to political polarization. They also explore the likelihood of political parties deciding to embrace AI to assist with campaigning—particular with individualizing persuasive messaging at scale—and how that could lead to an advantage in the polls.

In an article for Tech Policy Press, Faculty Associate Petra Molnar (https://cyber.harvard.edu/people/petra-molnar) and coauthors examine the rise of immigration detention technologies (https://www.techpolicy.press/technology-is-turning-immigration-detention-into-an-invisible-leash/) —including GPS monitoring and digital reporting tools—for monitoring immigration cases, despite a lack of regulation or thorough investigation of their impact on people. They outline guiding principles for rights-based, community-centered alternatives that continue to reduce physical detention while preventing psychological stress from 24/7 surveillance.

For more, visit the BKC website’s Community (https://cyber.harvard.edu/community) section, where writing (and more) from fellows, faculty associates, affiliates, and staff is aggregated.
