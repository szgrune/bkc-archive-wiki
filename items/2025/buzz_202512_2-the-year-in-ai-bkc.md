# The Year in AI @ BKC

**Source:** [Archived online](http://eepurl.com/juHpeA) · **Published:** 2025-12-18 · **By:** The Berkman Klein Center · **Feed tags:** berkman-buzz

---

Cheery group photo of the BKC staff and fellows, captioned "Happy New Year!"

Dear Buzz readers,

What a year it’s been! AI has continued to dominate the headlines as new systems, deals, lawsuits and policy initiatives have rolled out over the course of 2025. Generative AI tools and systems are increasingly permeating our lives—from how we learn, create, and work, to how (and with what) we connect. These developments are accentuating fundamental questions about why and how AI is designed and deployed, what’s under the hood of AI systems, who should govern it and how, what role(s) should (or shouldn’t) it play in our society, and what impact it is having on the core experience of being human at this time.

As we have through various evolutions of the internet, BKC exists to wrestle with these questions and help figure out how AI can serve people and the public interest. So, at BKC, we have been diving headlong into these questions over the past year—in our classic interdisciplinary and entrepreneurial way—through convenings, speakers, education, research, and building.

And though this edition of the Buzz is focused on BKC’s work on AI, I’d be remiss in not bidding another fond farewell to our successful Institute for Rebooting Social Media (https://rebootingsocialmedia.org/) , and celebrating the tremendous work across the Center: from the Applied Social Media Lab’s development of new products (many of which embrace AI) to improve our online experience to our student, executive education, and Community programming to the operational activities that enable all of BKC’s work. Along the way, we’ve striven to offer opportunities for you all to engage with the Center, and we always welcome ideas of how we can do this even better.

All of this work, and the energy generated around it, has been thanks in part to your interest and participation in BKC and its programming. From the bottom of my heart, I want to thank you all for your support and curiosity over the last year (and beyond). Ideas and projects are moving at BKC.

The Buzz is taking a break for the holidays. See you in 2026!

—Alex Pascal

** Expanding an AI agenda
------------------------------------------------------------

To turbo-charge our research, BKC appoint (https://cyber.harvard.edu/story/2025-07/josh-joseph-joins-berkman-klein-center-chief-ai-scientist) of Josh Joseph (https://cyber.harvard.edu/people/joshua-joseph) as its first Chief AI Scientist. Under his leadership, the Center advanced a research portfolio encompassing agentic AI, language model interpretability, benchmarking AI beyond just intelligence, and AI and the human experience. This work also includes the AI Triad, an initiative that aims to foster dialogue among the divergent approaches to responsible AI development of accelerationists, safety-oriented researchers, and skeptics, as featured in the Center’s AI Triad: A Dialogue Across Differences (https://cyber.harvard.edu/events/ai-triad-dialogue-across-differences) event, moderated by Faculty Director Jonathan Zittrain. Over the fall, Joseph began assembling a dedicated research team, with plans to hire research engineers and scientists in the coming year. This team is already
collaborating closely with BKC fellows, faculty associates, and affiliates, while building institutional partnerships across Harvard, including with the School of Engineering and Applied Sciences (SEAS (https://seas.harvard.edu/) ) and the Harvard Data Science Initiative (HDSI (https://datascience.harvard.edu/) ).

Through the Applied Social Media Lab (ASML), BKC technologists continued to develop AI-enabled tools to support democratic deliberation. ASML’s NextSpace (https://asml.cyber.harvard.edu/nextspace/) , an event-focused AI system, generates real-time summaries to help participants follow complex discussions, while its Event Assistant supports moderators with live synthesis to enable more constructive facilitation. Both were piloted during BKC events in 2025, with plans to expand in 2026.

Also, in 2025, Professor Lawrence Lessig and BKC launched (https://cyber.harvard.edu/story/2025-04/asml-invites-open-source-collaborators-to-frankly) Frankly (https://app.frankly.org/space/frankly) , a video-based discourse platform designed to support structured dialogue and collaborative decision-making across diverse groups. Open-sourced over the summer, Frankly incorporates AI-driven agenda formation and has attracted contributions from civic technology leaders, including Audrey Tang, Taiwan’s first Minister of Digital Affairs. The platform was demonstrated at the DelibTech Summit and Democracy R&D (https://demnext.substack.com/p/announcing-the-delibtech-network) in Brussels, and the National Roundtable on Political Violence (https://www.newsweek.com/national-roundtable-on-political-violence-10922212) , organized by AllSides and Newsweek, which gathered (https://www.newsweek.com/a-roundtable-on-political-violence-brings-strangers-together-to-break-echo-chambers-11012116) 637
participants and 151 breakout rooms across three sessions. The roundtables are planned to continue into 2026. Check out a roundup (https://cyber.harvard.edu/story/2025-12/news-roundup-asmls-frankly) of all the recent coverage of the events.

** Convening critical dialogue
------------------------------------------------------------

Through its spring (https://cyber.harvard.edu/story/2025-02/berkman-klein-announces-spring-speaker-series) and fall (https://cyber.harvard.edu/story/2025-10/berkman-kleins-fall-speaker-series) speaker series, BKC convened nearly 3,000 participants across approximately 30 events that explored the frontiers of current AI research and governance. Topics ranged from uncertainty and reasoning in machine learning, to AI and personhood, to intelligence, national security, and algorithmic collusion. Featured speakers included Jacob Andreas (https://cyber.harvard.edu/people/jacob-andreas) , Jamie Boyle (https://cyber.harvard.edu/node/91026) , E. Glen Weyl (https://cyber.harvard.edu/people/e-glen-weyl) , Blaise Agüera y Arcas (https://cyber.harvard.edu/people/blaise-aguera-y-arcas) , Finale Doshi-Velez (https://cyber.harvard.edu/people/finale-doshi-velez) , and Amba Kak (https://cyber.harvard.edu/people/amba-kak) .

The Center also hosted special guests throughout the year, including New York Times journalist Kashmir Hill (https://cyber.harvard.edu/people/kashmir-hill) , who joined ASML’s Meg Marco (https://cyber.harvard.edu/people/meg-marco) and BKC’s Jordi Weinstock (https://cyber.harvard.edu/people/jordi-weinstock) to discuss the dangers of sycophancy (https://cyber.harvard.edu/events/friend-flatterer-or-foe-psychology-and-liability-chatbots) , and former New Zealand Prime Minister Jacinda Ardern (https://www.hks.harvard.edu/centers/cpl/leaders-in-practice/hauser-leaders-program/bios) , who reflected (https://hls.harvard.edu/today/at-berkman-klein-ardern-urges-people-to-expect-more-from-elected-leaders/) on leadership, political violence, and the societal implications of emerging technologies. Don’t miss the most recent speaker series highlight reel! (https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=AhcoUF8JM6o)

Beyond public programming, BKC convened smaller expert forums to examine emerging research. In the spring, interpretability researchers Martin Wattenberg (https://cyber.harvard.edu/people/martin-wattenberg) and Fernanda Viégas (https://cyber.harvard.edu/people/fernanda-viegas) presented their dashboard (https://yc015.github.io/TalkTuner-a-dashboard-ui-for-chatbot-llm/) at a workshop that brought together researchers, technologists, and practitioners focused on the technical and policy implications of AI interpretability. Check out a new blog (https://cyber.harvard.edu/story/2025-12/black-box) about their work!

Harvard students from across disciplines also engaged with AI research and governance through groups such as Student Leaders in AI (SLAI (https://cyber.harvard.edu/projects/student-leaders-ai) ) and a speaker series cohosted by BKC and the AI Student Safety Team (AISST (https://cyber.harvard.edu/events/bkc-x-aisst-event-series-spring-2025) ).

** Teaching, training, engagement
------------------------------------------------------------

BKC faculty integrated AI research into classroom instruction, including an agentic AI course (https://hls.harvard.edu/courses/agentic-artificial-intelligence-and-the-law/) co-taught by Jonathan Zittrain and Josh Joseph. We’ve welcomed new colleagues to the team and refreshed educational initiatives, including our AI and the Law: Navigating the New Legal Landscape (https://go.execed.law.harvard.edu/l/868411/2025-11-25/n8k6mx) executive education program, which will be offered in May 2026 with a new emphasis on experiential learning.

In 2025, ASML staff presented tools at major convenings, including the World Wide Web Consortium’s Technical Plenary in Kobe, Mozilla Festival in Barcelona, and the Global Digital Collaboration Conference in Geneva. These efforts connected BKC’s applied research with practitioners advancing public-interest technology worldwide.

The BKC community also rallied around the Center’s ambitious AI research agenda. The 2025 fellows will be working alongside faculty leading specific AI research initiatives such as agentic AI and interpretability. Haley Song (https://cyber.harvard.edu/people/hayley-song) , for example, examines the geometric foundations of AI interpretability and safety, while Sean McGregor (https://cyber.harvard.edu/people/sean-mcgregor) explores how we might better understand AI risks by building the capacity to insure them. We also welcomed nearly 100 new (https://cyber.harvard.edu/story/2025-09/bkc-welcomes-2025-26-new-community-members) and returning BKC community members from across the world to the Center in Cambridge to kick off the new academic year, build and strengthen relationships across our network, and connect people to projects at the Center.

As always, the BKC community at large was working to address AI’s thorniest questions through essays, op-eds, research articles, and books, fostering critical dialogue in a polarized landscape. Zittrain unpacked BKC faculty associates Fernanda Viégas and Martin Wattenberg's interpretability work in The Atlantic’s "What AI Thinks It Knows About You" (https://www.theatlantic.com/technology/archive/2025/05/inside-the-ai-black-box/682853/) and probed the LLM-induced cognitive dissonance between what we want and what we think we should want with Jon Favreau in his “Offline” podcast (https://crooked.com/podcast/ai-zittrain-chatgpt-llms-openai/) . In a piece (https://www.techpolicy.press/forget-me-forget-me-not-memories-and-ai-agents/) for Tech Policy Press, Joseph and Kevin Frazier explored the vulnerabilities of AI agents’ memory and argued that the issue warrants further examination (https://www.techpolicy.press/forget-me-forget-me-not-memories-and-ai-agents/) . In another paper, Joseph and
research coordinator Seán Boddy addressed the risks (https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.22735) that misalignment and loss of control pose to increasingly complex LLM-based agents. And in the CommonWealth Beacon, Executive Director Alex Pascal and affiliate Nathan Sanders called for resistance (https://commonwealthbeacon.org/opinion/mass-must-resist-congresss-proposed-moratorium-on-state-ai-regulation/) against a moratorium on state AI regulation. The BKC community’s vast library of publications, press engagements, and public projects can be found online at cyber.harvard.edu/community.

Regardless of your real age or Spotify age, we are grateful you spent 2025 with us, and we are excited to share more with you in 2026.
