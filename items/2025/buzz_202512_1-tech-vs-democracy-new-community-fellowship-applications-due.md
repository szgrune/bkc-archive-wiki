# tech vs democracy; new community; fellowship applications due

**Source:** [Archived online](http://eepurl.com/jtyKbU) · **Published:** 2025-12-04 · **By:** The Berkman Klein Center · **Feed tags:** berkman-buzz

At a recent conversation hosted by metaLAB (at) Harvard and moderated by Sarah Newman, Electronic Frontier Foundation Executive Director Cindy Cohn and HLS Professor of Practice Larry Schwartztol mapped the widening tensions between technology, democracy, and the rule of law.

Citing the DOGE litigation, the fragility of federal privacy protections, the escalating risks of protest surveillance, government access to private data, and the limits of remedies like police body-worn cameras, Cohn and Schwartztol warned that concentrated platform power and the normalization of pervasive tracking are straining today’s legal frameworks—whether the Privacy Act, Section 230, or antitrust.

Yet they also stressed that the crisis in digital governance is a moment of opportunity: for rethinking the structure of independent agencies, for building genuinely decentralized alternatives to the major platforms, and for imagining more public, open, and democratic approaches to AI. And despite some spirited disagreement, they agreed on one thing: safeguarding democracy will require long-horizon work, new ideas, and new leaders. As Cohn put it, “The book for how we fight this attack on our liberties hasn’t been written yet; you can help write it.”

** Speaker Series Highlights
------------------------------------------------------------

BKC’s Fall Speaker Series featured an array of different perspectives and asked probing and profound questions about the nature, purposes, function, and impact of AI technology. With uncommon depth, curiosity, and constructive disagreement, speakers explored the “intelligence,” uncertainty, governance, and collective futures of AI.

Highlight Reel – BKC Fall Speaker Series on AI (https://www.youtube.com/shorts/AhcoUF8JM6o)

Our highlight reel captures the intellectual sweep of those conversations. Across the series, speakers wrestled with why AI resists fully consistent specification, how our governance frameworks evolve in practice rather than principle, what accountability means as these systems enter high-stakes domains and penetrate deeper into our lives, and how both human and machine intelligence might be better understood as emerging from interaction and collective structures rather than as isolated entities.

Taken together, the reel reflects what made this year’s Speaker Series so resonant: a willingness to embrace complexity, to surface and interrogate the assumptions beneath AI models and human policies, and to invite the kind of discourse that helps us better understand technology, and ourselves.

There’s little doubt that digital technology has transformed our lives, from forming new communities to daily scrolling rituals across social media platforms. But has it become a religion? Affiliate Greg Epstein (https://cyber.harvard.edu/people/greg-m-epstein) thinks so: “not just a religion, but the dominant religion of our time”—and he believes it’s time to think more critically about whether tech has earned our faith.

Affiliate Aparna Balagopalan (https://cyber.harvard.edu/people/aparna-balagopalan) is also interested in technology and trust, specifically AI reliability in safety-critical settings such as healthcare. Her work considers how to gain a deeper understanding of how models fail and adapt decision-making accordingly. She also advocates for centering the voices of patients, clinicians, and other healthcare stakeholders in data collection.

** More from the BKC Community
------------------------------------------------------------

Aymar Jean Escoffery (https://cyber.harvard.edu/people/aymar-jean-escoffery) , a 2024-25 Rebooting Social Media Visiting Scholar and current Faculty Associate, released the report (https://www.madelab.org/programs/social-media-tech-solidarity-workshop) from his year at BKC’s Institute for Rebooting Social Media. The report covers insights from a two-day workshop with scholars, storytellers, and community organizers about how social media platforms support or hinder mobilization across cultural differences. Plus! Dr. Escoffery’s Reparative Media: Cultivating Stories and Platforms to Heal Our Culture (https://mitpress.mit.edu/9780262553261/reparative-media/) is due out this month, with an open-access edition coming early next year.

Faculty Associate Daniel Carpenter (https://cyber.harvard.edu/people/daniel-carpenter) and colleagues released a new working paper conceptualizing “dark speculation,” an approach to “[e]stimating catastrophic harms from frontier AI” that couples “‘qualitative’ work” and “quantitative underwriting analysis.” The process “offers a systematic approach to reason about extreme, low-probability events in frontier AI, tempering complacency and overreaction.” Read their paper on arXiv. (https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.21838)

For Bot Populi, Faculty Associate Paola Ricaurte (https://cyber.harvard.edu/people/paola-ricaurte) revised a recent conference talk: her Pluriversality of Knowledge in the Age of AI (https://botpopuli.net/pluriversality-of-knowledge-in-the-age-of-ai/) essays “pluriversality” as a resistance to epistemicide, genocide, and ecocide. “When we talk about AI’s flattening of knowledge, the proposed solutions tend to be technical…What I don’t hear is a conversation about what it actually means to homogenize humanity’s knowledge—how that homogenization happens, and what consequences it carries.”

Over the last couple of weeks, BKC Affiliates and frequent collaborators Bruce Schneier (https://cyber.harvard.edu/people/bschneier) and Nathan Sanders (https://cyber.harvard.edu/people/nathan-sanders) have published multiple (https://www.theguardian.com/commentisfree/2025/nov/23/ai-use-strengthen-democracy) pieces (https://contrarian.substack.com/p/how-to-build-ai-for-democracy) , including an outline in Lawfare of the “four pivotal choices facing public and private actors (https://www.lawfaremedia.org/article/like-social-media--ai-requires-difficult-choices) ” “about how to shape AI and its uses.” Comparing the hype and harms of social media to those of AI, they urge that “we intentionally navigate a path to a positive future for societal use of AI—before the consolidation of power renders it too late to do so.”

In Public Money, Private Secrets: Rethinking FOIA in the Age of Public-Private Governance (https://lpeproject.org/blog/public-money-private-secrets-rethinking-foia-in-the-age-of-public-private-governance/) , a post on the Law and Political Economy Project blog, HLS Clinical Instructor Mason Kortz (https://hls.harvard.edu/faculty/mason-kortz/) tackles the opacity of public-private partnerships and the prospects for increased transparency in the future. “If the public is meant to make informed choices about how collective resources are spent and regulated, it must have access to accurate and complete information about public-private interaction.”

For more, visit the BKC website’s Community (https://cyber.harvard.edu/community) section, where writing (and more) from fellows, faculty associates, affiliates, and staff is aggregated.
