** What Is Intelligence?
------------------------------------------------------------

What happens when you pair up two random AI programs? After millions of iterations, they stop being random and begin to replicate themselves.

That experiment is just one of the insights Google’s Blaise Agüera y Arcas shared at BKC’s Fall Speaker Series kickoff, drawn from his new book, What Is Intelligence?: Lessons from AI About Evolution, Computing, and Minds (https://mitpress.mit.edu/9780262049955/what-is-intelligence/) . His central claim:

“It is not a metaphor to say that the brain is a computer. They are not like computers; they are computers.”

In conversation with BKC Executive Director Alex Pascal, Agüera y Arcas contrasted the open-ended, cooperative processes of evolution with the commercial systems shaping AI today. But, he stressed, origins don’t determine outcomes: Just as personal computers and the internet moved beyond their military beginnings, AI’s trajectory will not be fixed by its starting point. He framed human-AI symbiosis as the next phase of evolution, but he resisted the question of whether it is ultimately “good.” “What ‘we’ is changes, and that’s okay,” he said.

The discussion also turned to interpretability and transparency, a research priority at BKC. Agüera y Arcas highlighted the promise of “doing neuroscience on models” to reveal novel structures of intelligence, while cautioning that “various forms of deception, including non-transparency of systems, feel to me like a problem.”

** More from the BKC Community
------------------------------------------------------------

Faculty Associate Anupam Chander (https://cyber.harvard.edu/people/anupam-chander) examined a question at the heart of today’s copyright debates: Does training AI models on unlicensed internet works amount to infringement? His analysis, spanning four jurisdictions, including the US and the EU, appears in the ACM Digital Library and in a write-up from the Foundation for American Innovation (https://www.thefai.org/posts/global-copyright-risks-for-ai-training) .

And while you’re at it, check out work from Faculty Associate Elissa Redmiles (https://cyber.harvard.edu/people/elissa-m-redmiles) and her coauthors on how everyday users judge GenAI art—research that sheds light on public intuitions about creativity and ownership in the digital age. Read their findings (https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3706598.3713338) in Proceedings of the 2025 CHI Conference on Human Factors in Computing Systems.

Faculty Associate Mailyn Fidler (https://cyber.harvard.edu/people/mfidler) published with the Internet Society on how internet fragmentation—and its misconceptions—are being used to project state power. Her piece, A New Chapter in Internet Fragmentation (https://pulse.internetsociety.org/blog/a-new-chapter-in-internet-fragmentation) , unpacks the political stakes behind a term often used loosely.

In the Bulletin of the Atomic Scientists, Faculty Associate Allison Stanger (https://cyber.harvard.edu/people/allison-stanger) warned of the democratic risks posed by Big Tech’s unaccountable power. Her essay, The AI Raj: How Tech Giants Are Recolonizing Power (https://thebulletin.org/2025/09/the-ai-raj-how-tech-giants-are-recolonizing-power/) , draws historical parallels to emphasize the urgency of reining in today’s dominant platforms.

Former Fellow Armando Guio Español (https://cyber.harvard.edu/people/armando-guio-espanol-0) described his experience working on AI policy in Latin America (https://revista.drclas.harvard.edu/against-the-odds-latin-americas-quiet-leadership-in-the-age-of-ai/) and outlined strategies aimed at strengthening the region’s leadership. Read his perspective in Harvard’s ReVista.

In a piece for Lawfare, Affiliate Bruce Schneier (https://cyber.harvard.edu/people/bschneier) argues that the rise in authoritarianism requires new approaches to digital threat modeling (https://www.lawfaremedia.org/article/digital-threat-modeling-under-authoritarianism) and risk assessment online.

For more, visit the BKC website’s Community (https://cyber.harvard.edu/community) section, where writing (and more) from fellows, faculty associates, affiliates, and staff is aggregated.
