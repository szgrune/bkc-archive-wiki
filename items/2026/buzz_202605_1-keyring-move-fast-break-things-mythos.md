** Digital Identity Symposium: ASML Presents Keyring
------------------------------------------------------------

“We were handed a problem nobody had solved. We had no UX patterns, no templates, no precedent. And we built something that a real person can pick up and use in seconds.”

Last month, BKC’s Applied Social Media Lab hosted an in-person launch event for Keyring (https://asml.cyber.harvard.edu/advanced-digital-identity/) , an open-source app designed to give users control over their digital identity. Keyring, developed in collaboration with the Linux Foundation, uses advanced cryptography to help users choose exactly which part(s) of their identity they wish to disclose to different platforms, services, and communities in a verifiable and privacy-preserving way.

Learn more about Keyring in this writeup from the Harvard Gazette (https://news.harvard.edu/gazette/story/2026/05/worried-about-how-online-firms-use-data-they-get-from-you/) . Watch a recording from the Keyring launch event here. (https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=CK-FRcIo_BM)

** Event Recap: Move Fast, Break Things
------------------------------------------------------------

Much attention has been paid to the US-China AI race. At “Move Fast, Break Things: The Policy Origins of Today’s AI Race (https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=da7EEKOmgDc) ,” Harvard Kennedy School Fellow Nur Laiq and historian Marc Aidinoff examined an earlier technological competition: the interstate tech race of the 1980s.

Drawing on archival research, Laiq traced how governors across the United States poured public resources into high-tech industries through tax incentives, deregulation, pension fund investment, and public-private partnerships, helping transform high technology from an industry into a structural political force.

Laiq argued that the policies and incentives of that earlier “silicon chase” offer a clearer lens for understanding today’s US-China AI race—from competition over semiconductor investment and data centers to the growing influence of major tech firms over public policy. Rather than a sudden geopolitical rupture, she suggested, today’s AI race is the latest phase of a decades-long political and economic project built around subsidizing and deregulating high technology in the name of growth, innovation, and national advantage.

** More from the BKC Community
------------------------------------------------------------

In a forthcoming paper, Faculty Associate Ifeoma Ajunwa (https://cyber.harvard.edu/people/iajunwa) proposes an Artificial Intelligence Act to create a federal agency, the Artificial Intelligence Commission (https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=6615258) . "The agency is meant to be a coordinative agency working with other agencies as appropriate but having final authority over all A.I. related concerns."

Faculty Associate Nishant Shah (https://cyber.harvard.edu/people/nishant-shah) writes in The Indian Express how the speed of development of AI products like Anthropic’s Mythos shows just how far the law has fallen behind in regulating AI (https://indianexpress.com/article/opinion/columns/best-both-sides-imessage-claude-mythos-late-regulation-ai-slowing-down-10664748/) . “The question of whether AI regulation should be heavy-handed or with a light-touch has arrived too late. Claude Mythos signals the end of the internet as a public utility, and the shrinking power that governments have in national and global regulation.”

Co-founder Jonathan Zittrain (https://cyber.harvard.edu/people/jzittrain) recently spoke at a panel event on AI and its impact on social interaction at Harvard’s Edmond and Lily Safra Center for Ethics. Read coverage from the Harvard Gazette here (https://news.harvard.edu/gazette/story/2026/04/if-youre-boring-its-good-to-know-that-youre-being-boring/) .

Faculty Associate Sasha Costanza-Chock (https://cyber.harvard.edu/people/scostanzachock) argues in CalMatters that hypothetical conversations about the kinds of harm that AI can do to children understate the harms that the technology has already wrought (https://calmatters.org/commentary/2026/04/artificial-intelligence-children-crosshairs-california/) , including the strike on the Shajareh Tayyebeh elementary school in Iran. “The question is not whether AI will harm ‘our’ children someday. The question is whose children are already being harmed. And will we demand accountability?”

What does Anthropic's Mythos, with its ability to autonomously find and weaponize software vulnerabilities, mean for the future of cybersecurity? Affiliate Bruce Schneier (https://cyber.harvard.edu/people/bschneier) and Barath Raghavan suggest in an article for IEEE Spectrum that Mythos is likely the first in a series of incremental steps toward long-term changes in the relationship between AI and security (https://spectrum.ieee.org/ai-cybersecurity-mythos) .

In a new academic article, Daji Landis, Faculty Associate Elettra Bietti (https://cyber.harvard.edu/people/ebietti) , and alum Sunoo Park examine tech companies’ “Security v. Interoperability” arguments (https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.04538) in antitrust and regulatory proceedings, and provide frameworks for lawyers and researchers to interrogate the strength of these arguments. Also, check out this Lawfare article by Landis further explaining these proposed frameworks (https://www.lawfaremedia.org/article/security-versus-interoperability--real-tension-or-false-dichotomy) .

Faculty Associate Mailyn Fidler (https://cyber.harvard.edu/people/mfidler) writes in SCOTUSblog about Chartie—a landmark case considering whether a police warrant to access geofence data violates the Fourth Amendment (https://www.scotusblog.com/2026/04/digital-location-data-heads-back-to-the-supreme-court-/) . Additionally, the HLS Cyberlaw Clinic filed an amicus brief (https://www.supremecourt.gov/DocketPDF/25/25-112/400416/20260309154438148_25-112_Amici%20Brief.pdf) in Chatrie for a coalition of technologists that included Faculty Associate Arvind Narayanan (https://cyber.harvard.edu/people/arvind-narayanan) and Affiliate Bruce Schneier (https://cyber.harvard.edu/people/bschneier) .

In Internet Society Pulse, Affiliate Maria Xynou (https://cyber.harvard.edu/people/maria-xynou) shares OONI data tracking the evolution of internet censorship (https://pulse.internetsociety.org/en/blog/2026/05/measuring-internet-censorship-challenges-trends-and-impact/) , indicating that “Internet censorship is becoming more sophisticated, targeted, and harder to detect, highlighting the importance of continuous measurement and advocacy to safeguard online access and digital rights.”

For more, visit the BKC website’s Community (https://cyber.harvard.edu/community) section, where writing (and more) from fellows, faculty associates, affiliates, and staff is aggregated.
