** Ben Buchanan on AI Policy and Governance
------------------------------------------------------------

Last week, Johns Hopkins University Professor and former White House Special Advisor for AI Ben Buchanan joined Executive Director Alex Pascal for a conversation on AI policy, national security, and the challenge of governing a transformative technology built largely in the private sector.

Buchanan framed the US-China dynamic not as a race with a fixed endpoint, but as a competition across three dimensions: inventing frontier AI systems; adopting them across economic and national security systems; and diffusing them globally. He argued that the democratic choke hold on advanced chip manufacturing remains a critical strategic lever, and warned that the Trump administration's shift toward selling advanced chips to China risks undermining that advantage. Additionally, he cautioned that the US is falling behind on energy infrastructure, noting that China has installed more than twice the solar capacity in a single year than the US’s entire solar capacity.

Buchanan also identified recursive self-improvement (the process of AI systems improving their own capabilities) as the most under-discussed issue in the AI policy landscape, and urged developers and policy makers to engage with the concept in order to estimate how soon we might hit that milestone.

** More from the BKC Community
------------------------------------------------------------

BKC Faculty Director Jonathan Zittrain coauthors this article outlining how law and legal principles can inform safe and ethical AI development (https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=6036657) . “While legal alignment is not a catch-all solution for the many challenges arising from AI, it is both independently important and supportive of complementary alignment research programs.”

In a conversation with The Harvard Gazette, Faculty Co-Director Rebecca Tushnet explains the legal challenges around regulating AI-generated deepfakes (https://news.harvard.edu/gazette/story/2026/01/how-ai-deepfakes-have-skirted-revenge-porn-laws/?mc_cid=5a279cdb0d&mc_eid=0684bd1a74) . “The question of liability for being able to generate them is one we just don’t have much experience with. It was possible to do a whole bunch of this in previous generations of digital tools. It’s just that the tools were largely in the hands of people who didn’t want to do that.”

Also in The Harvard Gazette, Affiliate Bruce Schneier discusses the impacts AI could have on democratic processes (https://news.harvard.edu/gazette/story/2026/01/what-if-we-used-ai-to-strengthen-democracy/?mc_cid=5a279cdb0d&mc_eid=0684bd1a74) , and how to navigate the need to govern this technology while keeping it publicly accessible. “We worry that AI, as it is practiced, will further concentrate power. It doesn’t have to be that way. AI could be democratizing, and we imagine a future where AI helps distribute power rather than concentrate it. And to me, that’s the future we want to try to move toward.”

Affiliate David Weinberger argues in Popular Science that AI could be a powerful tool for teaching young students how to engage in constructive conversations (https://www.popsci.com/technology/ai-can-teach-our-students-the-art-of-dialogue/) . “Used this way, AI is not a plagiarism machine but a tool for developing ideas by learning to explore them in conversation.”

Faculty Associate Anupam Chander is quoted in The New York Times on the recent TikTok deal (https://www.nytimes.com/2026/01/22/technology/tiktok-deal-oracle-bytedance-china-us.html) allowing a group of US investors—including Oracle—to buy the app from its China-based parent company following years of political pressure and national security concerns. “My worry all along is that we may have traded fears of foreign propaganda for the reality of domestic propaganda.”

BKC Fellow Sean McGregor launched the AI Verification & Evaluation Research Institute (https://www.averi.org/) (AVERI), a 501(c)(3) nonprofit aimed at facilitating third-party auditing of AI platforms to ensure safe and secure development.

For more, visit the BKC website’s Community (https://cyber.harvard.edu/community) section, where writing (and more) from fellows, faculty associates, affiliates, and staff is aggregated.
