** Encrypted Spaces
------------------------------------------------------------

Users store a huge amount of data online, but how often can we verify if that information is truly safe? As more work, communication, and coordination move into collaborative software, relying on platforms that don’t make privacy the norm becomes increasingly fraught.

The Berkman Klein Center’s Applied Social Media Lab (ASML) is thrilled to share Encrypted Spaces, which explores an architecture for collaborative applications where data is encrypted and operations are cryptographically verifiable. As ASML Principal Investigator James Mickens (https://cyber.harvard.edu/people/jmickens) explained, the project “allows users to access the benefits of cloud-based services (e.g., always-available data and cross-device syncing) without forcing them to reveal sensitive information to people outside of trusted groups.”

Developed in close collaboration with the Cryptography Group at Microsoft Research, the project includes an open-source repository (https://github.com/encrypted-spaces) and a technical whitepaper (https://encryptedspaces.org/whitepapers/encrypted-spaces.pdf) describing the encrypted spaces architecture, both available on its website (https://encryptedspaces.org/) .

Encrypted Spaces was recently showcased in a WIRED story (https://www.wired.com/story/signal-alums-release-encrypted-spaces-a-new-system-for-building-private-collaboration-apps/?_sp=82303c48-a81f-4a56-b1d2-5f00b6bd482e.1781187633544) , “Signal Alums Reveal ‘Encrypted Spaces,’ a System for Making Private Collaboration Apps,” and in Gizmodo (https://gizmodo.com/signal-veterans-want-to-encrypt-slack-google-docs-and-basically-every-other-app-2000771623) , which featured quotes from project lead and ASML Senior Engineer Nora Trapp (https://cyber.harvard.edu/people/nora-trapp) .

** Student Essay Winners Featured in Tech Policy Press
------------------------------------------------------------

** New series titled Agency, Trust, and Governance in the Age of AI
------------------------------------------------------------

In the spring, BKC called on students from across Harvard to share their perspectives on artificial intelligence and its implications for democracy and power, culture and creativity, and the future of work and learning.

Selected essays published in Tech Policy Press (TPP) explore a range of issues, including the costs of offloading cognitive skills (https://www.techpolicy.press/ais-catastrophic-risk-isnt-rogue-machines-its-cognitive-surrender/) to AI, how the technology amplifies existing harms like environmental racism and inequality in the supply chain (https://www.techpolicy.press/addressing-regulatory-arbitrage-in-the-ai-supply-chain/) and design (https://www.techpolicy.press/ai-didnt-kill-design-it-exposed-it/) , and how privacy risks (https://www.techpolicy.press/ai-privacy-and-the-hidden-architecture-of-harm-from-inference/) posed by AI systems that can infer sensitive personal information from seemingly mundane user data. TPP released the first batch of winning essays this week as part of a series titled Agency, Trust, and Governance in the Age of AI (https://www.techpolicy.press/topic/agency-trust-and-governance-in-the-age-of-ai/) .

Thank you to our collaborators at TPP and to the BKC community members who reviewed and helped to revise essay submissions.

** AI Alignment Love Match
------------------------------------------------------------

Library Innovation Lab (https://lil.law.harvard.edu/) Director and BKC affiliate Jack Cushman (https://cyber.harvard.edu/people/jcushman) really, really wants some AI alignment. So much so he has designed “The AI Alignment Love Match.” The quiz is inspired (by online quizzes of the early 2000s, of course) by the work of Jonathan Zittrain (https://cyber.harvard.edu/people/jzittrain) and others at BKC on the AI Triad (https://cyber.harvard.edu/teaching/debates-frontier-artificial-intelligence-governance-ai-triad) , three discursive camps of Accelerationists, Safetyists, and Skeptics, who share many of the same research questions about AI, but are “systematically disincentivized to talk to each other, and end up using incompatible language and missing each other’s key results—a shame when all three camps are trying to figure out how to make a vast technological shift better for humanity.”

So, where’s your love match? Read the blog (https://jackcushman.org/writing/triad-quiz/) behind the ~*totally scientific*~ quiz, where you can also see the results of chatbots taking it. And take the quiz yourself! (https://jackcushman.org/apps/triad-quiz/)

** More from the BKC Community
------------------------------------------------------------

BKC Co-Director Rebecca Tushnet (https://cyber.harvard.edu/people/rtushnet) , Cyberlaw Clinic Director Chris Bavitz (https://cyber.harvard.edu/people/cbavitz) , and BU Law’s Jessica Silbey tackled the copyright condundra of AI outputs in a discussion moderated by HLS student (and BKC research assistant) Matt Kristoffersen. Who owns AI-created works? Underlining the US Copyright Office’s past punts, Tushnet suggested that, “[b]ecause we decided to stop thinking about what level of human-machine interaction leaves the human in charge enough to deserve copyright, that’s why we’re struggling now.” Read more about the discussion and watch the video via Harvard Law Today. (https://hls.harvard.edu/today/copyright-law-struggling-to-parse-ais-ascendancy/)

In an op-ed in The Guardian, BKC affiliates Nathan Sanders (https://cyber.harvard.edu/people/nathan-sanders) and Bruce Schneier (https://cyber.harvard.edu/people/bschneier) critique Bernie Sanders' proposal for an AI sovereign wealth fund, praising the US Senator's public-spirited goals while arguing instead for an AI public option (https://www.theguardian.com/commentisfree/2026/jun/08/bernie-sanders-ai-sovereign-wealth-fund-plan) . The concept of a public option, they explain, “is for governments, be it federal or state, to establish publicly developed and operated AI models run by public institutions under democratic control.” (Also in The Guardian recently, Sanders and Schneier called attention to the staggering number of AI use cases (https://www.theguardian.com/commentisfree/2026/jun/15/ai-use-by-the-us-government-is-ballooning-and-the-lack-of-transparency-is-troubling) that the Trump administration’s Office of Management and Budget has planned to automate government processing through
AI, and Schneier weighed in on the quarrels about Anthropic’s Fable (https://www.theguardian.com/commentisfree/2026/jun/16/anthropic-fable-ai) .)

“Cybersecurity is experiencing mission creep (https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=4588977) ,” argues Faculty Associate Mailyn Fidler (https://cyber.harvard.edu/people/mfidler) in a forthcoming law review article that “surfaces the phenomenon of cybersecuritization and offers a novel framework for analyzing and critiquing it.” “If we continue to ignore [cybersecuritization],” Fidler writes, “we risk abdicating further responsibility for difficult choices to the trump card of cybersecurity.”

Keyring (https://asml.cyber.harvard.edu/advanced-digital-identity/) , a project recently launched by BKC’s Applied Social Media Lab as part of its advanced digital identity portfolio, is an open-source identity verification tool that requires something hard to come by in the age of constant password breaches: trust. In “Trust That Scales With the Stakes (https://www.proofstreet.xyz/trust-that-scales-with-the-stakes-inside-harvards-keyring/) ,” Proof Point spoke with both Principal Engineer Brendan Miller (https://cyber.harvard.edu/people/brendan-miller) and Senior Software Engineer Alberto Leon (https://cyber.harvard.edu/people/alberto-leon) in order to outline how Keyring built both technical and relational “trust signals” for secure digital identity verification.

The Open Observatory of Network Interference (OONI) released Abortion Access Denied: Investigating the Global Censorship of Women on Web (https://ooni.org/post/2026-women-on-web-blocked/) . The report, co-authored by BKC affiliate Maria Xynou (https://cyber.harvard.edu/people/maria-xynou) , emphasized the compounding impact of network interruptions: “As activists around the world fight for bodily autonomy while navigating increasingly hostile political environments, they are also forced to fight for their own visibility online.”

Faculty Associate Lauren Bridges (https://cyber.harvard.edu/people/lauren-bridges) introduces the UVA DIGS Lab’s Data Center Policy Database, which “aims to bring transparency around zoning, permitting, and regulating data centers and their impacts on communities”: “The regulatory landscape for data centers in the United States has shifted dramatically in recent years from a period of aggressive economic incentives to a phase of intense scrutiny, restriction, and community-led resistance.” Bridges summarizes the findings of the Data Center Policy Database in Tech Policy Press. (https://www.techpolicy.press/the-state-of-data-center-policy-in-the-united-states/)

BKC Fellow DZ Kalman (https://cyber.harvard.edu/people/dz-kalman) and Executive Director Alex Pascal (https://cyber.harvard.edu/people/alex-pascal) were both quoted in a Jewish Insider feature about how Jewish leaders, and faith leaders broadly, are contending with the impacts of artificial intelligence and the choices driven by those impacts. Read “As AI reshapes society, Jewish leaders grapple with what comes next.” (https://jewishinsider.com/2026/06/ai-rabbis-jewish-thinkers-moral-guardrails-judaism-torah/)
