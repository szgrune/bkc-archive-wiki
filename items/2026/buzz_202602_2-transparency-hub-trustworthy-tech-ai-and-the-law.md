** Making the Fine Print Visible: A Virtual Launch Event for ASML’s Transparency Hub
------------------------------------------------------------

Have you ever received a notice online that a privacy policy has changed, but you weren’t really sure how? Wouldn’t it be helpful if there was a way to see the changes over time?

Join BKC’s Applied Social Media Lab (ASML) on March 4 to launch Transparency Hub, a new platform designed to help people explore, compare, and better understand the data practices of consumer-facing social and technology applications.

Transparency Hub offers unprecedented access to public-facing documents from over 300 social and technology companies. By aggregating and archiving privacy policies, terms of service, transparency reports, and community guidelines all in one place, users can easily search, analyse, export and compare documents. The goal is to help consumers and researchers track changes in platform governance over time and across platforms.

** Julie Brill on Balancing Trustworthy and Innovative Tech
------------------------------------------------------------

How do we ensure that AI and other advanced technologies remain trustworthy—while still enabling innovation and broad economic and social benefit?

That question was at the heart of our fireside chat last Tuesday with former FTC Commissioner and Microsoft Chief Privacy Officer Julie Brill, moderated by BKC Executive Director Alex Pascal. In the conversation, Brill emphasized that trustworthy technology depends on a governance ecosystem—one where regulators, companies, and consumers each have a role to play, and where the “checks and balances” go well beyond any single rule or institution.

A few themes from the discussion:
* Clear, adaptable guardrails: governance needs to set real objectives and accountability—while remaining flexible enough to keep pace with rapid technological change.
* Governance across multiple levels: thoughtful regulation matters, but so do corporate policies, customer and partner expectations, and emerging technical and industry standards.
* Collaboration to build trust: multi-sector engagement is essential if we want technology to serve society well over the long term.

** More from the BKC Community
------------------------------------------------------------

Faculty Associate Jon Penney (https://cyber.harvard.edu/people/jpenney) has a new book out! Chilling Effects: Repression, Conformity, and Power in the Digital Age (https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/chilling-effects/22383D541B3BC45C9145E85DA4824E10) explores “the repressive and conforming effects of technology, state, and corporate power, and offers a roadmap of how to respond to their weaponization today and in the future.”

In Inquest, Faculty Associate Dasha Pruss (https://cyber.harvard.edu/people/dasha-pruss) writes about the effects of carceral AI and how we can start to protect ourselves from further surveillance in the future (https://inquest.org/carceral-ai-is-here-its-time-to-fight-back/) . “In a moment when we are all subjected to unprecedented levels of carceral surveillance, turning the algorithmic gaze back at the powerful oppressors is one of our best strategies for reclaiming power and accountability.”

“Prompt injection isn’t something we can fix in current LLM technology…By understanding promptware as a complex, multistage malware campaign, we can shift from reactive patching to systematic risk management, securing the critical systems we are so eager to build.” In Lawfare, Affiliate Bruce Schneier (https://cyber.harvard.edu/people/bschneier) and fellow security experts explains promptware injection attacks against LLMs (https://www.lawfaremedia.org/article/the-promptware-kill-chain) and how defenders can better protect models against harm.

For Harvard Business School Working Knowledge, Faculty Associate James Riley (https://cyber.harvard.edu/people/dr-james-riley-phd) outlined his work with Simon Friis surveying which professions and jobs people are comfortable (or not) automating (https://www.library.hbs.edu/working-knowledge/riley-performance-resistance-2026) : “‘Respondents are implying that there’s this social and moral boundary marking what we as people, as a species, need to continue to do,’ Riley says. ‘We’re going to need to have a sober conversation informed by some rigorous analyses around where these boundaries are and why.’”

Against the narrative that “centralized corporate control” over AI is a foregone conclusion, Faculty Associates Trebor Scholz (https://cyber.harvard.edu/people/trebor-scholz) and Mark Esposito (https://cyber.harvard.edu/people/mark-esposito) propose an alternative “solidarity stack” to AI’s current “vertically integrated ‘extraction stack.’” Their piece in the Stanford Social Innovation Review points up “several early efforts by communities, cooperatives, and public institutions…already reclaiming control over these economic layers, including earth and infrastructure, data, labor, and knowledge (https://ssir.org/articles/entry/artificial-intelligence-solidarity-ecosystem) .”

For more, visit the BKC website’s Community (https://cyber.harvard.edu/community) section, where writing (and more) from fellows, faculty associates, affiliates, and staff is aggregated.
