** Mapping the Burden
------------------------------------------------------------

With generative AI tools now broadly available, the creation and distribution of non-consensual intimate images (NCII) has become easier than ever. Despite legislative initiatives like the TAKE IT DOWN Act in the US, online reporting structures for removing harmful imagery from platforms remain opaque and difficult to navigate, increasing the likelihood of retraumatization for the survivors, inaction, and, ultimately, more abuse.

BKC is pleased to share “Mapping The Burden: Toward Survivor-Centered Reporting Systems and Policies for Non-Consensual Intimate Images,” a white paper co-authored by BKC Program Coordinator Alice Jo and Research Assistant Grace Harlan, and advised by BKC Affiliate Cailin Crockett. They analyze the NCII reporting interfaces and policies of seven major online platforms and call for structural reforms to mitigate the burdens placed on survivors.

Read the full white paper here. (https://asml.cyber.harvard.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/18/2026/06/NCII-WP.pdf)

** More from the BKC Community
------------------------------------------------------------

Chief AI Scientist Josh Joseph (https://cyber.harvard.edu/people/josh-joseph) is quoted in a Wall Street Journal article considering the pros and cons of chatbots' long memories (https://www.wsj.com/tech/ai/ai-memory-cd1de7f4) . Joseph notes that it can be difficult to track the subtle ways that an LLM's memory affects its outputs. “Say you mention you’re stressed about money in a conversation about something else entirely. Weeks later, you ask the chatbot for career advice, and it steers you toward higher-paying jobs rather than roles that might be a better fit, because it ‘knows’ you’re financially anxious. You would never know why the advice felt off, because the chatbot hasn’t flagged which memories it is drawing on.”

Affiliate Bruce Schneier (https://cyber.harvard.edu/people/bschneier) and Faculty Associate Jon Penney (https://cyber.harvard.edu/people/jpenney) note the chilling effects that the Trump administration's war on campus speech has had on student activists (https://theconversation.com/chilling-effects-of-trumps-war-on-free-speech-extend-far-beyond-campus-walls-and-thats-the-point-283113) , writing in The Conversation that “[students] are self-censoring and disengaging from campaign activism to avoid punitive measures.” This fear, Schneier and Penney argue, “is doing the administration's work for it.”

In response to the pope’s recent encyclical, Magnifica Humanitas, Fellow DZ Kalman (https://cyber.harvard.edu/people/dz-kalman) shares his thoughts on AI and religion, and the role religious leaders could have in AI policy. “Judaism’s smallness and decentralization allows for lots of little experiments with AI theology and AI policy. I want to see local religious leaders play with AI in both ideas and activities.” Read the blog post here. (https://www.jellomenorah.com/p/14-thoughts-on-the-popes-ai-encyclical)

The Trump-Xi summit in Beijing realized the leaders' mutual recognition of the limits we must impose on AI: “Both the United States and China share an interest in preventing the release of AI models with dangerous capabilities by non-state actors,” writes Faculty Associate Mark Esposito (https://cyber.harvard.edu/people/mark-esposito) . What's less clear, he writes for The Institute for Peace & Diplomacy, is whether the current moment will eventually produce verifiable treaties (https://peacediplomacy.org/2026/05/28/when-guardrails-become-the-stakes-ai-governance-and-the-beijing-summit/) .

Fellow Mara Bolis (https://cyber.harvard.edu/people/mara-bolis) spoke with BBC’s The World Tonight to discuss differences in women’s approach to AI and how they perceive the risks of this new technology. “When women engage with systems that they've largely been left out of creating, the products can feel foreign and even hostile at times.” Tune in at 31:36 for Bolis’ section of the episode. (https://www.bbc.com/audio/play/m002wkk0)

Faculty Associate Noah Giansiracusa (https://cyber.harvard.edu/people/noah-giansiracusa) argues in Slate thatAI consciousness is an unlikely outcome (https://slate.com/technology/2026/05/ai-consciousness-neural-networks-mathematics.html?pay=1779992513987) because AI relies on math and formulas. “Most people don’t look inside A.I. chatbots. But if they did, they would see that…an A.I. chatbot is just a mathematical flip-book.”

In a new article for The Yale Law Journal, Faculty Associate Vivek Krishnamurthy (https://cyber.harvard.edu/people/vkrishnamurthy) outlinesthe internet’s exclusion from transit law protections (https://yalelawjournal.org/article/the-internet-and-the-lost-law-of-transit) , which specify when goods and foreign persons must be permitted to traverse state borders and territories, arguing that these protections must be extended to the internet in order to preserve it as “a shared global resource.”
