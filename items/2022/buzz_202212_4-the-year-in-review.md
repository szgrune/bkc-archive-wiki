2022 in review

It’s the final BKC Buzz of 2022! We’re looking back at the work of the Berkman Klein Center’s fellows, visiting scholars, faculty associates, affiliates, directors, students, and staff. All the community’s endeavors won't fit in a single newsletter, but we hope this slice gives a sense for what we've been up to in our first year with Sue Hendrickson (https://cyber.harvard.edu/story/2021-12/berkman-klein-center-welcomes-susan-hendrickson-executive-director) as Executive Director. For a fuller picture, visit the Community (https://cyber.harvard.edu/community) section of the BKC website.

Plus 📣 a reminder that currently there are several open opportunities for joining BKC, including our annual Open Call for Fellowship Applications (https://cyber.harvard.edu/getinvolved/fellowships/2324Fellows) . For more, see the Join BKC (#join-bkc) section below.
https://rebootingsocialmedia.org/

BKC's Institute for Rebooting Social Media (https://rebootingsocialmedia.org/) (RSM) got fully underway this year, welcoming Visiting Scholars (https://cyber.harvard.edu/story/2022-04/inaugural-cohort-visiting-scholars-rebooting-social-media) and Assembly Fellows (https://rebootingsocialmedia.org/programs-projects/fellowship/2022-23-rsm-assembly-fellows/) , hosting a student reading group (https://rebootingsocialmedia.org/spring-2022-student-discussion-group/) and events like Joanne Cheung on Democracy and the Financialization of Social Networks (https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=984b_ob0Dd8) (cf. Sahar Massachi on treating social media like a city (https://www.technologyreview.com/2021/12/20/1042709/how-to-save-social-media-treat-it-like-a-city/) ). From announcement to closing, Elon Musk's acquisition of Twitter accelerated debates around social media governance and further validated RSM's raison d'être. Indeed, in June, Jonathan Zittrain argued that "We need methods of governing our civic
discourse (https://www.theatlantic.com/ideas/archive/2022/06/elon-musk-twitter-takeover-mark-zuckerberg/661219/) that aren’t as capricious, opaque, and unaccountable as a private owner’s can be." 💥

For the many publications and convenings from RSM's Directors, Visiting Scholars, and Fellows, visit rebootingsocialmedia.org (https://rebootingsocialmedia.org/spring-2022-student-discussion-group/) .

Content moderation was endlessly discussed in 2022, online and off. The BKC community was no exception. Julie Owono went on On Point to discuss NetChoice v. Paxton (https://www.wbur.org/onpoint/2022/09/30/how-a-texas-law-could-impact-first-amendment-rights-and-content-moderation-online) , Chinmayi Arun published Facebook's Faces (https://harvardlawreview.org/2022/03/facebooks-faces/) in HLR, and Vivek Krishnamurthy brought international legal frameworks that address propaganda (https://techpolicy.press/war-propaganda-and-international-law-a-conversation-with-vivek-krishnamurthy/) into the conversation. And Evelyn Douek pushed us to shift the dominant mental model of content moderation in a year-end mic drop: Content Moderation as Systems Thinking (https://harvardlawreview.org/2022/12/content-moderation-as-systems-thinking/) .

Major stories intersected many of the BKC community's areas of inquiry and expertise. Kendra Albert, Afsaneh Rigot, and Masha Alimardani addressed the women-led protests in Iran and big tech (https://www.nytimes.com/2022/09/30/opinion/tech-companies-iran.html)  in The New York Times. For Lawfare, David Nemer and Will Marks discussed Telegram use in Ukraine (https://www.lawfareblog.com/telegrams-embrace-contradiction) . Critic and prescient chronicler of all things “blockchain” and “crypto” (including FTX (https://www.mollywhite.net/etc/ftx-contagion) ), Molly White spoke with The Protocol (RIP) about her practice and approach (https://www.protocol.com/fintech/molly-white-web3-crypto-skeptic) .

Safety, trust, and risk were persistent concerns this year. The BKC community addressed technology as a vector for harm, particularly for vulnerable groups, in a range of contexts. Among her many activities and appearances, Alejandra Caballaro first warned that Tech Companies Are Not Ready for a Post-Roe Era (https://www.wired.com/story/tech-companies-not-ready-post-roe-era/) , then pointed up Remote Learning’s Dangers for LGBTQIA+ students (https://slate.com/technology/2022/02/remote-learning-danger-lgbtq-students.html) . Zahra Stardust, with Rosalie Gillett and Kath Albury, showed how Surveillance does not equal safety (https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/17416590221111827?journalCode=cmca) on dating apps. Kishonna Gray talked to The Digital Public Interest Collective Podcast about harassment in gaming culture (https://open.spotify.com/episode/7jFaROyCUMRgXONobj4VAh) . Sameer Hinduja published widely on cyberbullying and, with Justin W. Patchin and Ryan C. Meldrum, tracked the
connection between Digital self-harm and suicidality among adolescents (https://acamh.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/camh.12574) . And with support from the Cyberlaw Clinic and ARTICLE 19, Afsaneh Rigot drew on years of in-depth research to understand the role of digital evidence in the persecution of LGBTQIA+ people (https://cyber.harvard.edu/publication/2022/digital-crime-scenes) in Egypt, Lebanon, and Tunisia; she advocated Design from the Margins (https://www.belfercenter.org/publication/design-margins) to reduce harm and improve technologies for all.
https://cyber.harvard.edu/programs/research-sprints

The BKC Research Sprint (https://cyber.harvard.edu/programs/research-sprints) program continued apace, starting with a recap of Alternative Data Futures (https://cyber.harvard.edu/story/2022-02/what-we-learned-research-sprint-cooperative-data-governance) , our research sprint with The New School’s Platform Cooperativism Consortium. In 2022, specialized cohorts of early-career scholars delved into Takedowns and Transparency (https://cyber.harvard.edu/story/2022-07/toward-best-practices-around-online-content-removal-requests) , with the Lumen team, and Digital Identity in Times of Crisis (https://cyber.harvard.edu/projects/digital-identity-times-crisis) , with BKC, metaLAB, the Edgelands Institute, and AccessNow (output coming early 2023).

At the start of 2022, we celebrated a recent special issue of the Journal of Social Computing: Technology Ethics in Action: Critical and Interdisciplinary Perspectives (https://www.benzevgreen.com/tech-ethics/) . The issue was edited by Ben Green, and it emerged from the Ethical Tech Working Group at BKC, co-founded in 2017 by Mary Gray and Kathy Pham. Every contributor was a regular participant in the Working Group's weekly meetings between 2017 and 2020: Jasmine E. McNealy, Lily Hu, Elettra Bietti, Salomé Viljoen, Luke Stark, Aden Van Noppen, Joanne Cheung, Jonnie Penn, Jenny Ungbha Korn, Maya Malik, and Momin M. Malik, with intellectual and logistical support from every corner of the BKC community! Dive in here (https://www.benzevgreen.com/tech-ethics/) .
https://protocol.mlml.io/

In the spring, metaLAB (at) Harvard & FU Berlin curated Living by Protocol (https://protocol.mlml.io) , in which "Social media will be simultaneously the topic, platform, and space of experimentation." Others came at issues of technology and expression from different tacks. Kendra Albert joined Marketplace Tech to discuss the brief but complex legal history of code as free speech (https://www.marketplace.org/shows/marketplace-tech/why-the-first-amendment-also-protects-code/) , and Ruth L. Okediji explored the equity implications of the interactions between AI and IP in a keynote, The Policy Boundaries of Creative Machines (https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=J7sTlOUSbzk) .

Last but not least, BKC scholars use research and expert analysis to envision a more just and equitable world of digital technology—and contribute to practical efforts to get there. A sampling: Sandra Cortesi, Malavika Jayaram, Lionel Brossi, and Urs Gasser contributed to Colombia's AI Expert Mission (https://cyber.harvard.edu/story/2022-08/bkc-scholars-helped-guide-colombias-ai-policy-implementation-part-countrys-ai-expert) , which concluded this summer; BKC, Global Network Initiative, Carr Center for Human Rights Policy, and Edmond & Lily Safra Center for Ethics hosted a Consultation on Ethical and Rights-Respecting Technology (https://cyber.harvard.edu/story/2022-08/recommendations-us-national-action-plan-responsible-business-conduct-technology) for the Biden-Harris Administration’s National Action Plan on Responsible Business Conduct; and the Cyberlaw Clinic submitted a comment in response to a request from the FTC for feedback
(https://blogs.harvard.edu/cyberlawclinic/2022/11/14/comment-to-the-ftc-on-transparency-and-commercial-surveillance/) on an Advance Notice of Proposed Rulemaking related to commercial surveillance and data privacy. As Urs Gasser and Virgílio Almeida argued in a new paper (https://cacm.acm.org/magazines/2022/3/258905-futures-of-digital-governance/abstract) , "[T]he scale, scope, and speed of next-generation digital technologies...point toward a need for another type of innovation: innovation in the realm of governance."
