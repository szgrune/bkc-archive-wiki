http://www.digitallyconnected.org/digital-champions-empowering-young-people-with-disabilities/Digital Champions 2016
In 2014, Digitally Connected (http://www.digitallyconnected.org/)  initiated a global search for powerful thinkers, creators, and innovators who are transforming their environment and communities for the better. As either individuals or teams, these "Digital Champions" are generating social change by addressing difficult questions around issues such as gender, rights, participation and civic engagement, discrimination, and poverty.

This year, Digitally Connected is shining a spotlight on young people with disabilities who are using the Internet and digital technologies to empower themselves and others. Take a look at
the first five inspiring stories (http://www.digitallyconnected.org/digital-champions-empowering-young-people-with-disabilities/)  from this year's group. We would love to make sure that more voices are heard and are currently accepting suggestions online (https://harvard.az1.qualtrics.com/SE/?SID=SV_dnXmX7Ur8pXn0gJ) for new Digital Champions.

If you have ideas for or questions about this project, please email Sandra Cortesi at digitallyconnected@cyber.harvard.edu.

https://cyber.harvard.edu/people/EHargittai
Blaming Facebook for Trump's win.
In this piece (http://thehill.com/blogs/pundits-blog/presidential-campaign/307438-stop-blaming-facebook-for-trumps-election-win) for The Hill, Eszter Hargittai (https://cyber.harvard.edu/people/EHargittai) and Keith N. Hampton argue that social media is not to blame for Donald Trump's win. "Trump’s message of intolerance pushed him to victory, not social media algorithms. It may not be a coincidence that the same demographic that is most likely to be disconnected from the internet was most likely to vote for Trump," they write.

The blame-game in a post-Trump world.
"Ever since Trump became the president-elect one question seems to be on many people’s minds: Who is to blame? The answers to this question range from the electoral system to international influence but one particular scapegoat is more prominent than others: the internet," writes Jonas Kaiser (https://cyber.harvard.edu/people/jkaiser) in this post (http://www.hiig.de/blog/the-blame-game-in-a-post-trump-world/) for the HIIG blog.

The effectiveness of multistakeholderism.
In this post (https://www.diplomacy.edu/blog/effectiveness-multistakeholderism-kenya-ict-review-2016) for Diplo, Grace Mutung'u (https://cyber.harvard.edu/people/gmutungu) describes the highs and lows of multistakeholderism in Kenyan ICT policy. "In Kenya, collective decision-making has been restored through the Constitution of Kenya 2010, where public participation is entrenched as a national value and principle."

https://cyber.harvard.edu/people/ycordova
Messaging apps in Brazil: can scarcity unlock innovation?
"Not unlike some other countries, Brazil is transitioning, in several fields, from old technologies to Internet-based technologies which are faster, more reliable and efficient — as well as cheaper. And such transitions usually are a fertile ground for innovative behaviour, such as in the case of messaging apps," writes Yasodara Córdova (https://cyber.harvard.edu/people/ycordova) inthis Medium post (https://medium.com/@yaso/blocking-the-unforeseen-f8df5fd1a1db#.wbit4wmax) .

https://cyber.harvard.edu/people/bschneier
Priorities for the next four years.
In this blog post (https://www.schneier.com/blog/archives/2016/12/my_priorities_f.html) , Bruce Schneier (https://cyber.harvard.edu/people/bschneier) outlines his new agenda for the next four years, which includes continuing to solve problems and lay the groundwork for a better future while still "fighting the fights." "No matter how bad the next four years get," he writes, "I don't believe that a Trump administration will permanently end privacy, freedom, and liberty in the US."

https://cyber.harvard.edu/people/jgriffey
Libraries and the State of the Nation.
“In the face of the current political climate, with the use of opinions as bludgeons and disinformation as the weapon of choice for manipulation and intellectual coercion, it is up to those who value fact and believe in the care of those in need to stand up and positively affirm that to do otherwise is evil," argues Jason Griffey (https://cyber.harvard.edu/people/jgriffey) in this post (https://medium.com/berkman-klein-center/libraries-and-the-state-of-the-nation-146b9b72a95c#.hufattfao)  about libraries, neutrality, and action.

2017 is for the Attention Innovators.
“Our work in 2017 will require creative efforts — new experiments, new tools, new ways of telling stories, and new ways of sharing. It will require a better understanding of how people in power can wield attention in misleading, confusing and damaging ways,” writes An Xiao Mina (https://cyber.harvard.edu/people/axmina) in this piece (http://www.niemanlab.org/2016/12/2017-is-for-the-attention-innovators/) for Nieman Lab.

‘Born Digital’ Redux.
In this piece (http://today.law.harvard.edu/born-digital-redux/) for Harvard Law Today, Alba Hancock (https://cyber.harvard.edu/people/ahancock) reports on the recent publication of “Born Digital: How Children Grow Up in a Digital Age (http://www.basicbooks.com/full-details?isbn=9780465053926) ,” by John Palfrey and Urs Gasser. The book is an expansion of their celebrated 2008 book “Born Digital: Understanding the First Generation of Digital Natives.”
