Calling Internet infrastructure fans! Join us for:
Meeting 21st Century Municipal Internet Access Needs (https://cyber.harvard.edu/events/2017/04/BroadbandSummit)
Tuesday, April 25, 2017
10 a.m. - 12:30 p.m.
At this free public event, Jascha Franklin-Hodge, Boston's CIO, will describe the city's ongoing efforts at fostering private sector competition in providing high-speed wired and wireless Internet access. And Kevin Stokes, CIO of Brookline, will discuss the opportunities and challenges in trying to work across institutional and state agency boundaries to obtain fiber-optic network access to boost local bandwidth and reduce costs. Municipal and state officials are invited to attend and then participate in a discussion about best practices and opportunities for collaboration. The event will conclude with an audience Q&A and bag lunch.

http://www.aljazeera.com/indepth/opinion/2017/04/infrastructure-vulnerabilities-surveillance-easy-170409071533166.html
Infrastructure vulnerabilities make surveillance easy (http://www.aljazeera.com/indepth/opinion/2017/04/infrastructure-vulnerabilities-surveillance-easy-170409071533166.html)
Surveillance is far from a new technique. Computers, however, have made it easier than ever before. In this Al Jazeera story (http://www.aljazeera.com/indepth/opinion/2017/04/infrastructure-vulnerabilities-surveillance-easy-170409071533166.html) , Bruce Schneier (https://cyber.harvard.edu/people/bschneier) writes, "Computers naturally produce data about their activities, which means they're constantly producing surveillance data about us as we interact with them." Read the article (http://www.aljazeera.com/indepth/opinion/2017/04/infrastructure-vulnerabilities-surveillance-easy-170409071533166.html) to learn more about how corporations, governments, and other entities exploit vulnerabilities in our technology infrastructures.

Machines are now deriving "conclusions from models that they themselves have created, models that are often beyond human comprehension, models that 'think' about the world differently than we do," writes David Weinberger in this Backchannel post (https://backchannel.com/our-machines-now-have-knowledge-well-never-understand-857a479dcc0e) ; read on (http://) for more about machine learning, the knowability premise, and alien justification.
https://medium.com/@bracy/is-it-time-for-a-tech-sanctuary-movement-98851305f3c0
Is it time for a tech sanctuary movement? (https://medium.com/@bracy/is-it-time-for-a-tech-sanctuary-movement-98851305f3c0)
The sanctuary movement is becoming the center of a debate in the tech community, as policies become less welcoming toward immigrants, refugees, and other groups. In this Medium post (https://medium.com/@bracy/is-it-time-for-a-tech-sanctuary-movement-98851305f3c0) , Catherine Bracy (https://cyber.harvard.edu/people/cbracy) writes about a recent event that sought to address the relationship between technology organizations and the sanctuary movement.

https://hbr.org/2017/04/who-controls-the-blockchain
Who controls the blockchain? (https://hbr.org/2017/04/who-controls-the-blockchain)
In this article for the Harvard Business Review (https://hbr.org/2017/04/who-controls-the-blockchain) , Patrick Murck (https://cyber.harvard.edu/people/pmurck) explores the relationship between blockchain governance and decentralized infrastructure, arguing that "As a blockchain community grows, it becomes increasingly more difficult for stakeholders to reach a consensus on changing network rules...Blockchain networks resist political governance because they are governed by everyone who participants in them, and by no one in particular."
