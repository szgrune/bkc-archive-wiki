http://www.bostonglobe.com/ideas/2017/05/11/the-onslaught-spam-calls-will-keep-getting-worse/2w1tyrSnzEj8NPO81hUUBK/story.html
The onslaught of spam calls will keep getting worse (http://www.bostonglobe.com/ideas/2017/05/11/the-onslaught-spam-calls-will-keep-getting-worse/2w1tyrSnzEj8NPO81hUUBK/story.html)
If you've received a spam phone call in the last year, you're part of a group of 5.3 million Americans. David O'Brien (https://cyber.harvard.edu/people/dobrien) weighed in on the issue in the Boston Globe (http://www.bostonglobe.com/ideas/2017/05/11/the-onslaught-spam-calls-will-keep-getting-worse/2w1tyrSnzEj8NPO81hUUBK/story.html) , where he noted that consumers should be cautious with spam blockers: "With every new app that we download, we are introducing more people to the party and into our personal lives on the phone. We should be just as wary as to what we’re putting on our phones, even if it seems to be solving the problem.”

Digital inclusion and outcomes-based evaluation (https://www.benton.org/blog/digital-inclusion-and-outcomes-based-evaluation)
Early last year, Colin Rhinesmith (https://cyber.harvard.edu/people/crhinesmith) published a report on the offerings of digital inclusion organizations — which included research on affordable broadband and devices. A new Benton Foundation report (https://www.benton.org/blog/digital-inclusion-and-outcomes-based-evaluation) builds on Dr. Rhinesmith's work, and "describes the challenges facing community-based organizations...in using outcomes-based evaluation to measure the success of their digital inclusion programs."

http://reallifemag.com/the-apophenic-machine/
The Apophenic Machine (http://reallifemag.com/the-apophenic-machine/)
In this piece for Real Life Magazine (http://reallifemag.com/the-apophenic-machine/) , Molly Sauter (https://cyber.harvard.edu/people/msauter) discusses the connections we make between materials on the Internet — and how they can lead to conspiracy theories, like Pizzagate or ClimateGate. Sauter writes, "Humans are storytellers, pattern-spotters, metaphor-makers. When these instincts run away with us, when we impose patterns or relationships on otherwise unrelated things, we call it apophenia. When we create these connections online, we call it the internet, the web circling back to itself again and again. The internet is an apophenic machine."

http://technosociology.org/?p=1751
Twitter and Tear Gas: The Power and Fragility of Networked Protest (http://technosociology.org/?p=1751)
Zeynep Tufecki (https://cyber.harvard.edu/people/zeynep) 's recently published book, "Twitter and Tear Gas: The Power and Fragility of Networked Protest (http://technosociology.org/?p=1751) ," examines the relationship between online media and grassroots protest movements. Inside Higher Ed (https://www.insidehighered.com/blogs/library-babel-fish/when-internet-angry) said that "Social interactions in the digital world in the context of political activity is insightfully explored through this wonderfully readable academic study;" Slate (http://www.slate.com/articles/news_and_politics/interrogation/2017/05/zeynep_tufekci_author_of_twitter_and_tear_gas_on_networked_protest.html)  asked if protesting has gotten to easy to be effective; and Marketplace (https://www.marketplace.org/2017/05/12/elections/how-effective-are-networked-protests) spoke with Tufecki about her research.
